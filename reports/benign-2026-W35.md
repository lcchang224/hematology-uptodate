## Summary

This week's literature is dominated by hemophilia genetics, pediatric aplastic anemia, ITP B-cell immunology, and coagulation assay pharmacology, with comparatively modest new signal in TTP, PNH, and thalassemia. First, a *Blood* whole-genome sequencing study of 52 hemophilia A patients identifies four novel genetic modifiers of factor VIII (FVIII) clearance beyond the established STAB2/ASGR2/CLEC4M receptor loci — including a FUT2 Secretor-status variant (rs12979891) associated with prolonged FVIII half-life in non-secretors — while confirming that VWF levels, ABO group, and age together explain less than 40% of clearance variance, underscoring substantial unexplained pharmacokinetic heterogeneity relevant to individualized dosing[^1]. Second, a well-powered single-center pediatric randomized controlled trial from China (n=54) shows that adding the thrombopoietin receptor agonist hetrombopag to first-line immunosuppressive therapy for pediatric severe aplastic anemia does **not** significantly improve 6-month complete response (59.3% vs 62.9%, p=0.948) or 12-month response rates, contradicting the adult treatment paradigm and cautioning against routine upfront TPO-RA/IST combination in children outside those over age 9 showing an early 3-month CR signal (44.4% vs 27.3%, p=0.049)[^2]. Third, a *BJH* study of romiplostim-treated ITP patients demonstrates a mechanistic correlate of drug-free remission, showing a significant, durable expansion of anergic B cells (from 51.7% at baseline to 58.4% at 1 year, p=0.006, remaining elevated at 59.7% after tapering) alongside reduced IgG+/IgA+ antibody-secreting cells, offering the first B-cell biomarker candidate for TPO-RA-induced tolerance[^3]. Fourth, a multicenter *Blood Advances* study across 23 laboratories provides a comprehensive practical framework for interpreting coagulation assays in patients receiving factor XI(a) inhibitors (abelacimab, milvexian, asundexian), showing that aPTT is prolonged in a concentration-dependent fashion (greatest with milvexian) while prothrombin time and Clauss fibrinogen are unaffected, and that abelacimab uniquely produces persistently underestimated FXI clotting activity even after high-dilution correction — directly relevant as these agents progress toward clinical adoption[^4]. Fifth, real-world Japanese registry data (ADVANCE Japan) show that arterial thrombotic event incidence in aging hemophilia patients (4.67% over 5 years, 28/599 participants) is comparable to the general Japanese population, with the Hisayama cardiovascular risk model (sensitivity 70.5%, specificity 68.0%) outperforming QRISK3, which substantially overestimated risk in this population — informing primary prevention strategy as the hemophilia population ages[^5].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Beyond last week's mechanistic SRSF1-LCK splicing report, this week adds clinically translatable immunological data on TPO-RA mechanism of action. The *BJH* study by Nelson et al. performed longitudinal spectral flow cytometry on B cells from ITP patients before, during, and after romiplostim treatment[^3]. Anergic B cells constituted the predominant peripheral B-cell subset even at baseline in romiplostim-naïve ITP patients (mean 51.7%) and healthy controls (mean 47.8%), but paired longitudinal analysis showed these cells increased significantly during treatment, exceeding healthy-control levels after 1 year (mean 58.4%, p=0.006) and remaining stably elevated after drug tapering (mean 59.7%), regardless of whether patients remained treatment-free or resumed romiplostim. Concurrently, IgG+ and IgA+ antibody-secreting cells within the switched memory compartment decreased during treatment (p=0.04 and p=0.08, respectively). These findings suggest TPO-RAs exert an immunomodulatory effect on autoreactive B-cell populations that may partly explain the 30–50% sustained remission rate observed after TPO-RA discontinuation, and position anergic B-cell frequency as a candidate biomarker for predicting durable remission — a question not addressable with current clinical predictors.

## III. Aplastic Anemia

Following last week's EMAA trial in moderate aplastic anemia, this week's pediatric severe aplastic anemia (SAA) data provide an important contrast regarding TPO-RA utility across age groups. In a single-center, prospective, randomized trial, 54 children with newly diagnosed SAA were randomized 1:1 to standard immunosuppressive therapy (IST) alone or IST plus hetrombopag (HPAG)[^2]. At the primary 6-month endpoint, complete response rates were statistically indistinguishable between arms (59.3% vs 62.9%, p=0.948), and overall response rates were identical (85.2%) in both groups. At 12 months, ORRs remained identical (77.8%), and CRRs were numerically higher with IST alone (74.1% vs 62.9%, p=0.875, not significant). Time to response did not differ. A signal of benefit was confined to children older than 9 years, in whom 3-month CRR was higher with combination therapy (44.4% vs 27.3%, p=0.049), suggesting a possible age-dependent interaction that warrants further subgroup-focused study. Eight grade ≥3 adverse events, primarily transaminitis, occurred with combination therapy but were all manageable without discontinuation, and clonal evolution rates were comparable between arms (23.8% vs 26.3%). These results argue against routine upfront TPO-RA/IST combination therapy for most pediatric SAA patients, diverging from the adult evidence base (including last week's EMAA data in moderate AA) and highlighting that pediatric marrow failure biology may not simply mirror adult disease. Complementing this, a Japanese real-world administrative-data study characterizes the safety and effectiveness of TPO-RAs in aplastic anemia in routine clinical practice, though detailed comparative outcome figures were not available in the abstract[^6].

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

This week's most mechanistically important hemophilia development is the whole-genome sequencing study of FVIII pharmacokinetic modifiers in 52 patients with hemophilia A[^1]. Building on known associations between FVIII clearance and STAB2, ASGR2, and CLEC4M clearance receptor variants, plasma VWF levels, ABO blood group, and age, the investigators found these established factors collectively explained less than 40% of observed clearance variance. Four novel signals emerged: rs4904820 (TC2N), rs3124768 (ADAMTS13), rs12979891 (RASIP1/MAMSTR/FUT2), and rs6494314 (C2CD4B), each significantly associated with FVIII PK parameters. Of particular translational interest, the FUT2 locus determines Secretor blood group status, governing ABO(H) glycan secretion into plasma; non-secretor individuals demonstrated prolonged FVIII half-life compared with homozygous secretors, and this was corroborated functionally, as Fut2 knockout mice showed significantly increased plasma FVIII levels. Given that plasma FVIII level is itself a dose-dependent cardiovascular risk factor, these findings carry direct implications both for individualized hemophilia A dosing and for broader cardiovascular risk stratification. Complementing this genetic work, the ADVANCE Japan registry analysis addressed a distinct clinical question: as the hemophilia population ages, how should arterial thrombotic risk be estimated[^5]? Among 599 Japanese adults with hemophilia (median age 59 years, 83% hemophilia A, 63% severe disease) followed for 5 years, 28 first arterial events occurred (4.67% cumulative incidence). The Hisayama model achieved sensitivity 70.5%/specificity 68.0%, the Suita score achieved 73.3%/61.2%, and QRISK3 achieved 71.4%/57.7%; critically, observed event rates (2.93 per 100 participants over 5 years) closely matched predictions from the Hisayama model and Suita score, while QRISK3 substantially overestimated risk, and incorporation of hemophilia-specific clinical factors did not improve predictive performance beyond these established general-population models. Separately, a *Haematologica* correspondence piece addresses interpretation of short-term emicizumab use in acquired hemophilia A, arguing that observed treatment durations in prior reports are shaped by remission-driven discontinuation patterns rather than reflecting intrinsic drug limitations[^7], a nuance relevant to clinicians managing this rare autoimmune bleeding disorder.

## VIII. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

A *BJH* retrospective cohort study reports that sirolimus is effective for pediatric Evans syndrome and secondary autoimmune cytopenias, adding to the growing evidence base for mTOR inhibition as a steroid-sparing strategy in refractory pediatric autoimmune cytopenias, though detailed response figures were not available in the abstract provided[^8]. This aligns with an emerging treatment paradigm distinguishing Evans syndrome pathobiology, which frequently involves broader immune dysregulation, from isolated ITP or AIHA.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

A *Blood Advances* physiological study in elite ultra-endurance athletes provides mechanistic insight relevant to exercise-associated functional iron deficiency, distinct from but complementary to last week's erythroid FGF23 findings[^9]. In 7 athletes completing an 86.9 km mountain trail run with 6500 m of ascent, extreme exertion induced a marked inflammatory response (elevated interleukin-6) paralleled by increased hepcidin and ferritin, while serum iron and transferrin saturation declined — a classic functional iron deficiency signature. Peripheral blood mononuclear cell transcriptional profiling showed upregulation of hypoxia-inducible and iron uptake genes alongside downregulation of iron storage and export genes, with biochemical evidence of concurrent intravascular hemolysis. These findings reinforce that inflammation-driven hepcidin induction, rather than absolute iron deficiency, underlies exercise-associated anemia risk in extreme endurance settings, with practical implications for pre-race iron supplementation strategies in athletes. Separately, a *Blood Advances* commentary critically reassesses the evidence base underpinning ferric carboxymaltose use within the broader IV iron pharmacopeia, though without new outcome data in the abstract available[^10].

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

The most practically important coagulation development this week is the multicenter assessment of laboratory assay interference by three factor XI(a) inhibitors in clinical development — abelacimab (parenteral monoclonal antibody), and the oral small molecules milvexian and asundexian[^4]. Across 23 laboratories testing spiked plasma at concentrations spanning phase 2/3 trial ranges (50–2000 ng/mL for milvexian/asundexian; 1–30 μg/mL for abelacimab), prothrombin time, Clauss fibrinogen, and clotting activities of FII, FV, FVII, and FX were unaffected in a clinically meaningful way. Activated partial thromboplastin time was prolonged in a concentration-dependent manner, most pronounced with milvexian, followed by abelacimab and asundexian, while clotting activities of FVIII, FIX, FXI, and FXII were underestimated — an artifact correctable by high plasma dilution (up to 1:160) for the small molecules, but persistent for abelacimab, whose FXI clotting activity remained falsely decreased even at low concentrations (2.5 μg/mL). Lupus anticoagulant testing via dilute Russell viper venom time was unaffected, but LA-sensitive aPTT reagents risked false-negative results, and protein C anticoagulant activity was systematically overestimated. This granular data set gives laboratories a practical framework for result interpretation as FXI(a) inhibitors move toward approval. Additionally, this week's *NEJM* review of myeloproliferative neoplasms, while primarily a malignant-hematology topic, reiterates that inflammation-driven clonal dominance is a key contributor to the thrombotic complications characteristic of JAK2/CALR/MPL-mutated MPNs, relevant background for hematologists managing MPN-associated thrombosis in clinical practice[^11]. Separately, a *Haematologica* single-center retrospective study characterizes bleeding, thrombosis, and anti-Xa monitoring patterns in danaparoid-treated patients with suspected heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, though detailed outcome figures were not available in the abstract[^12], and a companion mechanistic *Blood* paper identifies Jmjd1c-mediated epigenetic dysregulation in B cells as a novel driver of PF4/heparin-specific antibody production underlying HIT pathogenesis, providing the first evidence linking epigenetic regulation to this drug-induced immune disorder[^13].

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Alpha-thalassemia carrier prevalence in Taiwan remains approximately 4–5% and beta-thalassemia carrier prevalence approximately 1–2%, sustaining the ongoing national premarital and antenatal carrier screening programs coordinated through NHI-supported laboratories; no new thalassemia-specific data appeared this week to modify current screening or transfusion practice at NCKUH. G6PD deficiency, prevalent at an estimated 3–5% in the Taiwanese population with a Hakka/Aboriginal predominance in some regions, continues to warrant screening prior to neonatal and pediatric use of oxidative drugs; this week's iron/hemolysis findings from endurance athletes are of tangential relevance for counseling G6PD-deficient recreational athletes regarding exertional hemolysis risk, though no direct G6PD data were published this week. Regarding hemophilia care, NHI coverage in Taiwan currently supports standard-half-life and select extended-half-life FVIII/FIX concentrates as well as emicizumab for hemophilia A with inhibitors; this week's FVIII clearance genetics data[^1] may in the future inform Taiwan-based pharmacokinetic-guided dosing protocols already piloted at several transplant/hemophilia comprehensive care centers (e.g., NTUH, CGMH), while the ADVANCE Japan cardiovascular risk-model comparison[^5] is directly relevant given the demographic similarity of the aging Taiwanese hemophilia cohort and the feasibility of applying validated East Asian population risk calculators (e.g., Suita-like models) rather than Western tools such as QRISK3. For ITP, romiplostim and eltrombopag remain NHI-reimbursed for chronic refractory disease at NCKUH and peer centers; this week's B-cell biomarker findings[^3] may eventually support biomarker-guided tapering discussions in clinic, although prospective validation in a Taiwanese cohort would be needed before practice change. No new PNH-specific developments this week affect current eculizumab/pegcetacoplan or iptacopan reimbursement pathways under Taiwan's NHI catastrophic illness provisions.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The principal message this week is a divergence between adult and pediatric aplastic anemia treatment paradigms: while last week's EMAA trial supported eltrombopag addition to CSA in adult moderate aplastic anemia, this week's pediatric RCT of hetrombopag plus IST in severe pediatric disease showed no overall benefit, restricting any signal to a small subgroup of children over age 9. Clinicians should resist extrapolating TPO-RA/IST combination benefit from adult to pediatric SAA populations pending further data. In hemophilia, the identification of FUT2 Secretor status and three additional novel loci as FVIII clearance modifiers, alongside validated cardiovascular risk modeling from the ADVANCE Japan cohort, both push toward more individualized, genotype- and population-informed management as this patient population ages. In ITP, the demonstration of durable anergic B-cell expansion during romiplostim treatment offers the field's first mechanistic correlate for TPO-RA-induced remission, a finding that merits prospective biomarker validation. Finally, the comprehensive FXI(a) inhibitor coagulation assay interference dataset provides essential practical guidance that Taiwanese hematology laboratories should incorporate proactively ahead of anticipated clinical adoption of abelacimab, milvexian, and asundexian.

## Journal Literature — CrossRef

Key non-malignant articles reviewed this week beyond those discussed above include a *Blood* mechanistic study of interleukin-4 binding to platelet glycoprotein VI, demonstrating that IL-4 inhibits collagen-induced platelet activation and attenuates arterial/venous thrombosis in vivo, revealing a novel anti-inflammatory brake on thrombus formation with potential therapeutic implications for thrombotic disorders[^14]. A companion *BJH* paper describes an updated diagnostic comparison for pediatric autoimmune neutropenia, showing that cell-based granulocyte agglutination/immunofluorescence testing detects anti-CD16 antibodies in 36% of suspected cases versus only 19% by bead-based LabScreen Multi assay, with pro-LL-37 and absolute neutrophil count improving diagnostic discrimination[^15]. A retrospective analysis of sickle cell disease and commercial aviation characterized 84 pre-flight and 76 in-flight SCD-related medical events, finding vaso-occlusive complications during air travel to be uncommon and current preventive measures generally adequate[^16].

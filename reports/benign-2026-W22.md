## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, the first-in-human lentiviral α-globin gene therapy case report in *Blood Advances* describes a 14-year-old girl with transfusion-dependent HbH disease (--SEA/αCSα) who achieved transfusion independence from week 4, sustained a mean haemoglobin of approximately 90 g/L at months 12–24, and maintained HbA at >85% of total haemoglobin with stable vector copy numbers and no insertional mutagenesis[^1]. Second, a *Blood* landmark review marks the 25th anniversary of ADAMTS13's discovery, synthesising the therapeutic advances — from rituximab and caplacizumab in iTTP to recombinant ADAMTS13 (rADAMTS13) as the new gold standard for congenital TTP — and delineating future directions including rADAMTS13 expansion to iTTP and next-generation ADAMTS13 assays[^2]. Third, a *Blood* proteomics study identifies clusterin as a Weibel-Palade body (WPB) component that stabilises von Willebrand factor (VWF) high-molecular-weight multimers by competitively inhibiting ADAMTS13-mediated proteolysis; clusterin-knockout mice develop haemorrhagic diathesis rescued by exogenous clusterin, and reduced plasma clusterin is demonstrable in type 2A von Willebrand disease patients[^3]. Fourth, a multicentre randomised open-label phase 2 trial in *BJH* demonstrates that dexamethasone plus glycyrrhizinate achieves a 6-month overall response rate of 56.25% in newly diagnosed ITP versus 31.25% with dexamethasone alone (p = 0.0231), with a longer duration of response and an acceptable safety profile, introducing a novel adjunct to first-line ITP therapy[^4]. Fifth, a *Blood Advances* retrospective cohort of 121 acquired pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) cases across 14 European centres identifies cyclosporine A (CyA) as the most effective immunosuppressive agent (ORR 61%, complete response 47%), finds mTOR inhibitor responses of 71% in CyA-refractory cases, and reports a 30% overall mortality predominantly driven by infectious complications — highest in MDS-associated PRCA[^5].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

A multicentre randomised open-label phase 2 trial (NCT05023915) published in *BJH* by Wang et al. enrolled patients aged ≥18 years with newly diagnosed, treatment-naïve ITP and baseline platelet count <30 × 10⁹/L, randomising them 1:1 to dexamethasone plus glycyrrhizinate (n = 55 enrolled; 48 in modified ITT) or dexamethasone monotherapy (n = 56 enrolled; 48 in modified ITT)[^4]. The primary endpoint — overall response at month 6, defined as platelet count ≥30 × 10⁹/L, at least doubling of baseline platelet count, and no bleeding — was achieved in 56.25% (27/48) of the combination arm versus 31.25% (15/48) of the monotherapy arm (p = 0.0231). No difference in response was observed at the early timepoint of day 14, suggesting that glycyrrhizinate exerts a delayed immunomodulatory effect on platelet destruction rather than an acute mechanism. The combination arm also demonstrated a longer duration of response. No grade 3–5 adverse events or deaths occurred; treatment-emergent adverse events were predominantly gastrointestinal reactions and anxiety. Glycyrrhizinate — the active constituent of liquorice root with known anti-inflammatory and potential thrombopoietic properties — has a plausible mechanistic rationale in murine ITP models, though phase 3 confirmation and long-term remission data are needed before this combination can be adopted into practice.

A separate *Haematologica* study by Lucchini et al. provides important real-world data on autoimmune cytopenias (AICs) arising after alemtuzumab-induced renal transplantation, drawing from a single-centre 8-year retrospective analysis[^6]. Among 40 patients who developed AICs, 28 had ITP, 7 had autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA), and 5 had Evans syndrome, with two temporal peaks of incidence at 18 and 36 months post-transplant. The response to rituximab in the ITP subset was notably high at 91.6%, with a median duration of response of 56 months, exceeding rates typically reported in primary ITP. Half of patients receiving thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) were able to discontinue treatment and maintain remission — also higher than the primary ITP benchmark. Clinically, infections (62.5%), cardiovascular events (27.5%), and deep vein thrombosis (25%) were the most frequent adverse events, underscoring the imperative to maintain antimicrobial prophylaxis and reintroduce antithrombotic therapy promptly after platelet recovery. These data suggest that the immunological milieu following alemtuzumab-mediated lymphodepletion may paradoxically predispose to autoreactivity while also conferring greater sensitivity to B-cell-depleting and thrombopoietic therapies.

## II. TTP / HUS / Thrombotic Microangiopathy

A landmark review article published in *Blood* by Scully, Carter, and Subhan marks the 25th anniversary of ADAMTS13's discovery and provides the most authoritative synthesis to date of its clinical and therapeutic evolution[^2]. The authors trace how identification of ADAMTS13 in 2001 catalysed the development of clinical ADAMTS13 activity assays enabling rapid, accurate differentiation of immune TTP (iTTP) from congenital TTP (cTTP) and other TMAs, transforming a disease with historical mortality exceeding 90% into one with current acute-episode survival approaching 90%. Two landmark additions to iTTP therapy are detailed: anti-CD20 treatment with rituximab — both in acute and elective (pre-emptive) settings to prevent relapse by targeting the anti-ADAMTS13 IgG-producing B-cell clone — and caplacizumab, the anti-VWF nanobody that blocks platelet–VWF interaction, which reduces time to platelet response and organ-damage accrual. For cTTP, the authors highlight recombinant ADAMTS13 (rADAMTS13, BAX930) as the current gold standard, enabling both prophylactic and on-demand enzyme replacement without the thrombotic risk of plasma infusions. Looking forward, the review delineates the potential for rADAMTS13 to expand into iTTP (as adjunct to immunosuppression during the period of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency), next-generation rapid ADAMTS13 assays to improve emergency-room diagnosis, and the role of ADAMTS13 deficiency in non-TTP thrombotic contexts including COVID-19-associated coagulopathy and cardiovascular disease. This review will serve as the definitive reference for teams building institutional TTP protocols.

Mechanistically complementary to the ADAMTS13 review, Qiao et al. in *Blood* identify clusterin — a multifunctional plasma glycoprotein previously linked to apoptosis and complement regulation — as a novel WPB-resident protein that maintains haemostatic homeostasis by protecting VWF high-molecular-weight (HMW) multimers from ADAMTS13-mediated proteolysis under shear stress[^3]. Using proteomics of endothelial secretomes and co-transfection assays, clusterin was confirmed to co-localise with VWF within WPBs. Mechanistically, clusterin binds the D4N domain of VWF, sterically inhibiting ADAMTS13 cleavage at the Tyr1605-Met1606 bond. Clusterin-knockout mice exhibit haemorrhagic diathesis and impaired arterial thrombosis associated with loss of HMW VWF, effects rescued by exogenous clusterin administration. Crucially, plasma clusterin was significantly reduced in patients with type 2A von Willebrand disease — a disorder characterised by pathological loss of HMW VWF — suggesting clusterin deficiency may be a co-pathogenic mechanism. A companion editorial by Ng in *Blood* contextualises this finding within the broader paradigm of WPBs as regulatory hubs[^7]. The clinical implication is a potential novel therapeutic strategy of VWF multimer stabilisation (rather than factor replacement) for certain VWD subtypes.

## III. Aplastic Anemia

A *Blood* study by Duan et al. examines how L-carnitine-driven fatty acid oxidation (FAO) governs haematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) function using a low-input mass spectrometry-based metabolomics platform across 13 immunophenotypically defined haematopoietic cell populations from adult human bone marrow[^8]. The platform detected over 80 metabolites using as few as ~10,000 cells per sample. L-carnitine emerged as a key metabolic feature of HSPCs, mechanistically activating the PPARA-TFEB signalling axis to promote mitochondrial metabolism and autophagy preservation of regenerative capacity. Functional assays in primary CD34+ HSPCs confirmed that L-carnitine supplementation ex vivo improved reconstitution in both healthy donors and — critically — patients with aplastic anaemia. In vivo transplantation experiments further supported enhanced regenerative fitness following L-carnitine treatment. These findings introduce a potentially low-cost, clinically accessible metabolic intervention as a priming or adjunct strategy for HSPC-based therapies in bone marrow failure, though prospective clinical validation in aplastic anaemia patients is required before any practice change.

Separately, a brief case report in *Blood* by Holland and Boothby describes thymoma-associated aplastic anaemia with concurrent Good syndrome — a rare concurrence of anterior mediastinal thymoma, haematopoietic failure, and hypogammaglobulinaemia with B-cell lymphopenia[^9]. Although detailed quantitative data are not available in the indexed abstract, this case adds to the literature supporting systematic immunological evaluation (including immunoglobulin levels and B-cell subsets) in aplastic anaemia patients with mediastinal abnormalities, a point also reinforced this week by the BJH Shwachman-Diamond syndrome immunological profile study (detailed in Section IX below).

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

An EHA Congress abstract by Dr. Karin Fijnvandraat indexed in the web-news feed addresses inhibitor development in mild haemophilia A — a clinically important complication in which the risk of inhibitor formation is paradoxically related to treatment intensity and the structural divergence of therapeutic FVIII from the patient's endogenous (partially functional) protein[^10]. The abstract itself is not available in full-text indexed form this week, but the topic is of direct relevance to NCKUH clinicians managing mild haemophilia A patients undergoing elective procedures who require FVIII concentrate rather than desmopressin. The risk of inhibitor formation in this setting is concentrated in patients carrying missense mutations encoding FVIII proteins with residual activity in the C1 and C2 domains, and current ISTH guidance recommends minimising unnecessary concentrate exposure and considering desmopressin as a first-line haemostatic agent wherever haemostatic response is adequate. The ISTH–EAHAD partnership agreement on bleeding disorders (2025) further signals upcoming collaborative guideline activity in this area[^11].

## VI. Von Willebrand Disease

The clusterin-VWF study by Qiao et al. described in detail under Section II has direct implications for understanding the pathophysiology of type 2A von Willebrand disease and for developing VWF multimer-stabilising therapeutics[^3]. In the clinical cohort reported by the authors, plasma clusterin protein levels were significantly reduced in type 2A VWD patients compared with healthy controls, raising the possibility that quantification of plasma clusterin may serve as a supplementary biomarker of HMW VWF loss, or that recombinant clusterin administration could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for patients with this ADAMTS13-susceptible VWD subtype. No data yet exist on clusterin levels in types 1, 2B, 2M, or 2N VWD, nor in acquired VWD — all of which warrant investigation.

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

The most significant development in thalassemia this week is the first-in-human report of lentiviral α-globin gene therapy for transfusion-dependent Hb H disease by Yin et al. in *Blood Advances*, representing a milestone with direct relevance to Taiwan given the high carrier frequency of --SEA deletional α-thalassaemia in our population[^1]. The patient — a 14-year-old girl carrying the --SEA/αCSα genotype (Hb H disease with constant spring mutation, representing one of the most severe non-deletional Hb H variants) — had been transfusion-dependent prior to enrolment in NCT05851105. Autologous CD34+ HSPCs were mobilised with G-CSF plus plerixafor, collected by apheresis, and transduced with the lentiviral vector Lenti-HBA (encoding wild-type α-globin under the endogenous HBA2 regulatory elements) at an average vector copy number sufficient for clinical expression. Following conditioning with intravenous busulfan, cells were reinfused with engraftment confirmed at three weeks. Transfusion independence was achieved from week 4 onward. During months 12–24 of follow-up, the mean haemoglobin was approximately 90 g/L — a level consistent with a non-transfusion-dependent state for Hb H disease — with HbA constituting >85% of total haemoglobin by HPLC. Vector copy numbers remained stable, with no evidence of clonal dominance, insertional mutagenesis, or replication-competent lentivirus on sequential monitoring. Adverse events were limited to expected busulfan toxicities, notably grade 2 menstrual irregularity and ovarian failure (classified as a serious adverse event). Despite cessation of chelation therapy, iron overload progression was minimal with no cardiac iron deposition on T2* MRI. Immune reconstitution, growth, and development were preserved at 24-month follow-up. This case establishes proof-of-concept for lentiviral α-globin gene therapy as a curative option for transfusion-dependent HbH disease and will be closely watched by our thalassaemia programme, particularly given the absence of available matched sibling donors in a substantial proportion of our Taiwanese HbH patients.

A *BJH* study by Thubthed et al. examines the mechanism by which activated platelets induce neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and neutrophil–platelet aggregation in β-thalassaemia/haemoglobin E (HbE) patients — a genotype with particular prevalence in Southeast Asia and among the Thai/Southeast Asian immigrant community in Taiwan[^12]. Using three-dimensional confocal microscopy on samples from splenectomised and non-splenectomised β-thal/HbE patients and normal controls, the authors demonstrate that platelet-borne P-selectin and HMGB1 bind to PSGL-1 and RAGE on neutrophils respectively, triggering NET formation through MEK/ERK, NOX2, MPO, and PAD4 signalling pathways. In splenectomised patients specifically, the U0126 MEK inhibitor (but not direct ERK inhibition) suppressed web-like NET formation and cell aggregation with associated antioxidant effects, suggesting that the ROS pathway represents the dominant mechanistic node driving thrombosis in post-splenectomy β-thal/HbE — and identifies ROS as a potential therapeutic target. This work extends the pathophysiological understanding of hypercoagulability in non-transfusion-dependent thalassaemia, particularly in the high-thrombotic-risk post-splenectomy subgroup, where current antiplatelet and anticoagulant strategies remain empirical.

## VIII. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

Data from the Lucchini et al. *Haematologica* study on alemtuzumab-induced autoimmune cytopenias (described in detail under Section I) are also relevant to AIHA practice[^6]. Among 7 AIHA cases and 5 Evans syndrome cases in this cohort, response and relapse rates to standard first-line therapy (corticosteroids) were comparable to primary AIHA forms. Rituximab and TPO-RAs showed good efficacy. The notably high deep vein thrombosis rate of 25% across the AIC cohort — encompassing both thrombocytopenic and haemolytic patients — reinforces the clinical imperative for prompt anticoagulation in AIHA patients with prothrombotic risk factors (e.g., Evans syndrome with resolved thrombocytopenia, or underlying renal transplant-related thrombophilia), a management nuance not always reflected in standard AIHA guidelines written for the primary setting.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis)

A *Blood* study by Ling et al. provides novel mechanistic insight into Diamond-Blackfan anaemia (DBA) pathogenesis by demonstrating that GATA1 N-terminal domain loss — characteristic of the short GATA1s isoform associated with DBA and myeloid leukaemia of Down syndrome — causes aberrant glycolytic reprogramming through upregulation of pyruvate kinase M (PKM), in addition to the previously characterised haematopoietic transcription defects[^13]. Using single-cell RNA sequencing on fetal liver cells from GATA1s-mutant embryos, the authors identified aberrant upregulation of PKM and other glycolytic genes. CUT&RUN and nuclear run-on sequencing after acute GATA1 deletion confirmed PKM as a direct GATA1 target. GATA1s substitution induced histone lactylation at the PKM promoter, increased PKM expression and activity, and enhanced glycolytic flux without affecting mitochondrial respiration. Critically, PKM expression was also elevated in DBA patients carrying RPS19 mutations (which reduce GATA1 levels), reinforcing the mechanistic link between GATA1 deficiency, glycolytic dysregulation, and erythropoietic failure. These findings have therapeutic implications: the pyruvate kinase activator mitapivat — approved for pyruvate kinase deficiency and under investigation in haemoglobin disorders — may merit exploration in DBA, where aberrant PKM upregulation rather than deficiency represents the primary lesion. However, the directional difference (excess PKM activity in DBA versus insufficient PKM activity in PK deficiency) would predict divergent rather than convergent pharmacological requirements, and careful mechanistic modelling is needed before clinical translation.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

A large retrospective cohort study by Sohail et al. in *Blood* utilised the TriNetX Research Network (2000–June 2025) to evaluate the safety of intravenous (IV) iron administration in adults with iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) during five categories of acute infection: MRSA bacteraemia, pneumonia, UTI, colitis, and cellulitis[^14]. After propensity matching (1:1), survival was significantly higher at both 14 and 90 days in patients who received IV iron across all infection categories (p < 0.001 for each). In the MRSA bacteraemia subgroup — arguably the highest-risk scenario for which caution about IV iron has been most loudly expressed — 14-day survival was 97.6% versus 95.0% and 90-day survival was 88.6% versus 83.8% in favour of IV iron. Haemoglobin recovery at 60–90 days was significantly greater with IV iron across all subgroups (p < 0.001), with incremental haemoglobin gains of +1.3 to +1.5 g/dL versus +0.7 to +1.0 g/dL in controls. While the retrospective design and unmeasured confounders (including antibiotic regimen, infectious severity scores, and baseline inflammatory markers) preclude definitive causal inference, these data represent the largest and most diverse real-world dataset addressing this longstanding clinical controversy. The findings challenge the entrenched clinical reflex to defer IV iron in infected, anaemic patients until infection has fully resolved, and support a reassessment of current clinical pathways — particularly in hospital patients with pneumonia or UTI, where the yield of early IV iron may be substantial.

A *Blood* study by Zeng et al. identifies biallelic loss-of-function mutations in BPNT1 as a novel cause of vitamin B12-dependent megaloblastic anaemia in three patients with recurrent disease[^15]. Mechanistically, BPNT1 deficiency causes accumulation of 3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphate (PAP), impairing ribosome biogenesis and reducing ileal expression of the cubam receptor complex (comprising amnionless [AMN] and cubilin [CUBN]) in Bpnt1-null mice, thereby disrupting intestinal cobalamin absorption. This discovery expands the differential diagnosis of recurrent B12-dependent megaloblastic anaemia beyond the classic causes (pernicious anaemia, dietary deficiency, Imerslund-Gräsbeck syndrome due to cubilin/AMN mutations) and has direct clinical implications: patients with unexplained recurrent B12-responsive megaloblastic anaemia who test negative for anti-intrinsic factor antibodies and have normal gastric histology should be evaluated for BPNT1 biallelic variants, particularly in families with consanguinity.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

The tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) study by Maroney et al. in *Blood* demonstrates that TFPI plays an essential, temporally specific role in embryonic cerebrovascular angiogenesis that is only partially compensated by activated protein C[^16]. Tfpi-knockout mice develop lethal embryonic cerebrovascular lesions (glomeruloid bodies) associated with excess thrombin generation; a transgene producing hyperactivatable mouse protein C reduced glomeruloid body numbers by only 36% and did not prevent fibrin deposition or blood-brain barrier disruption within lesions, though cytoprotective effects contributed to embryonic survival. Bulk RNA sequencing of embryonic day 15.5 brain tissue identified increased angiogenesis — including upregulation of apelin, adrenomedullin, and UNC5b — as the predominant biological process altered by TFPI deficiency. The findings define TFPI as an irreplaceable regulator of thrombin generation during embryonic vascular development, with implications for understanding thrombophilic states associated with TFPI deficiency and the potential embryonic risks of TFPI-targeted haemostatic agents currently in clinical development (e.g., concizumab, marstacimab) in patients of childbearing potential.

A *BJH* review by Lê et al. provides a comprehensive mechanistic synthesis of thrombocytosis and platelet-derived microparticle (PMP) formation in sickle cell disease (SCD)[^17]. The authors detail how thrombocytosis — common in SCD, particularly in response to vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, and chronic haemolysis — generates activated platelets that release PMPs containing phospholipids, tissue factor, and inflammatory mediators. These PMPs enhance thrombin generation, entrap leucocytes, promote endothelial adhesion, and drive vaso-occlusion in the microvasculature in a self-amplifying cycle. Therapeutic strategies targeting platelet activation — including aspirin, P2Y12 antagonists, and anti-P-selectin antibodies — are discussed in the context of ongoing and planned SCD trials. This review is directly relevant to the Section XII trial tracker for SCD.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings this week have direct implications for clinical practice at NCKUH and affiliated centres. The first-in-human lentiviral α-globin gene therapy report for transfusion-dependent HbH disease is of immediate strategic relevance to Taiwan's national thalassaemia programme[^1]. The --SEA deletion is the most prevalent pathogenic α-thalassaemia allele in Taiwan, with a carrier frequency of approximately 4–5% in the general population. HbH disease (genotype --SEA/α⁻ or --SEA/αCSα) is not rare in our clinics, and a meaningful proportion of the most severely affected patients (particularly those with --SEA/αCSα or --SEA/αQS) lack HLA-matched sibling donors for allogeneic HSCT. The NCT05851105 lentiviral platform offers a potentially curative autologous option, and NCKUH haematologists should consider direct engagement with the trial investigators to assess eligibility criteria and potential collaborative enrolment pathways.

The NET-thrombosis study in β-thalassaemia/HbE patients[^12] has clinical relevance for the Southeast Asian immigrant population in Taiwan — now exceeding 800,000 residents — among whom compound heterozygous β-thal/HbE is encountered with increasing frequency in haematology clinics. Splenectomised patients in this cohort are at the highest thrombotic risk and represent a subgroup for whom structured anticoagulation and platelet-targeted prophylaxis protocols should be formalised. NHI coverage for hydroxyurea in non-transfusion-dependent thalassaemia remains available under the rare disease programme; however, anticoagulation in this population is managed heterogeneously across centres.

The IV iron during acute infection study[^14] has direct implications for the high volume of anaemic patients with concurrent infection seen in Taiwan's tertiary care hospitals, where multidrug-resistant MRSA bacteraemia is a recognised challenge. The finding of improved 90-day survival (88.6% vs. 83.8%) with IV iron even in MRSA bacteraemia — the most conservative scenario — will require prospective validation before guidelines change, but clinicians should be aware that the data currently do not support automatic deferral of IV iron in all infected patients.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The most practice-influencing development this week is the first-in-human α-globin lentiviral gene therapy case achieving durable transfusion independence at 24 months in HbH disease — a genotype highly prevalent in Taiwan and for which curative options have been limited to allogeneic HSCT. The ADAMTS13 25th anniversary review consolidates the therapeutic framework for iTTP (rituximab + caplacizumab) and cTTP (rADAMTS13) while charting next-generation diagnostics and potential expansion of rADAMTS13 to iTTP, and should be read by all clinicians involved in TTP management. The clusterin-VWF biology reframes WPBs as haemostatic regulatory platforms and identifies a novel mechanism of HMW VWF protection with translational potential in VWD subtypes characterised by pathological ADAMTS13-mediated cleavage. For ITP, the dexamethasone-glycyrrhizinate phase 2 data are hypothesis-generating and justify a larger confirmatory trial, though the magnitude of benefit (56% vs. 31% OR at 6 months) is clinically meaningful if reproducible. The PRCA cohort analysis establishing CyA as the most effective first-line agent (61% ORR) and mTOR inhibitors as credible second-line options (71% response in CyA-refractory disease) represents the largest evidence base to date for this rare entity and should inform our PRCA management protocols at NCKUH.

## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a *Blood* case series by Shu et al. reports the first clinical evaluation of BCMA-directed CAR-T cell therapy in four patients with refractory primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), demonstrating rapid and sustained platelet recovery with durable drug-free complete remission lasting up to 15 months and a manageable safety profile — establishing proof-of-concept for a B-cell–depleting cellular therapy approach in a disease previously confined to thrombopoietin receptor agonists and immunosuppression[^1]. Second, a *BJH* review by Afolabi et al. provides a structured clinical framework for managing hereditary antithrombin deficiency in pregnancy, characterising this as the highest-risk inherited thrombophilia in the obstetric setting and detailing individualised anticoagulation strategies incorporating low-molecular-weight heparin and selective antithrombin concentrate replacement to optimise maternal and fetal outcomes[^2]. Third, a *BJH* multicentre retrospective cohort study by Dahan et al. evaluates high-dose eltrombopag (above standard 75 mg/day ceiling) in adult ITP, providing novel efficacy and safety data for dose escalation in patients refractory to standard thrombopoietin receptor agonist dosing[^3]. Fourth, a *Haematologica* review by Franchini, Mohsenian, and Mannucci synthesises international registry and biochemical data on rare inherited coagulation disorders, demonstrating that deficiencies of FXII and FXI may be paradoxically protective against thrombosis while select dysprothrombinemias, dysfibrinogenemias, FXIII deficiency, and afibrinogenemia confer genuine thrombotic risk — a paradigm shift with direct implications for anticoagulation decision-making in these patients[^4]. Fifth, a *Blood* study by Jewell et al. reconceptualises factor V as an endogenous anticoagulant of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, demonstrating in plasma from people with haemophilia A that procofactor FV inhibits tissue factor:FVIIa-mediated FX activation by up to 50–80% at physiological concentration through a phosphatidylserine-dependent membrane mechanism — potentially explaining how natural FV variation modifies bleeding and thrombotic phenotypes in haemophilia A[^5].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Two important new studies in ITP were published this week, each addressing distinct and previously underserved clinical scenarios.

**BCMA CAR-T in Refractory ITP.** A first-in-disease case series by Shu et al. published in *Blood* reports the clinical outcomes of four patients with primary ITP refractory to multiple prior lines of therapy who received BCMA-directed CAR-T cell therapy[^1]. All four patients achieved rapid platelet recovery, and drug-free complete remission was sustained for up to 15 months in the longest-followed case. Cytokine release syndrome was manageable across the cohort, and no severe immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity was reported. The biologic rationale for targeting BCMA in ITP rests on depletion of long-lived plasma cells responsible for producing anti-platelet autoantibodies — an approach conceptually distinct from anti-CD19 CAR-T (which targets mature B cells and early plasma cells) and from small-molecule strategies such as thrombopoietin receptor agonists that bypass the autoimmune driver entirely. While the case series is small and no formal response-rate statistics are available in the current data extract, these results provide compelling proof-of-concept for larger prospective evaluation. Given that BCMA-directed CAR-T has already demonstrated the capacity to induce drug-free serological and clinical remissions in systemic lupus erythematosus and myositis, this report aligns ITP with the emerging paradigm of cellular therapy for refractory autoimmune cytopenias. For practitioners at NCKUH managing patients with multiply refractory chronic ITP, this study justifies referral discussions for compassionate-use or early-phase trial access as programmes are established in Taiwan's major academic centres.

**High-Dose Eltrombopag in Adult ITP.** A *BJH* multicentre retrospective cohort by Dahan et al. evaluates the efficacy and safety of eltrombopag doses exceeding the standard approved ceiling of 75 mg/day in adults with chronic ITP who had failed standard-dose thrombopoietin receptor agonist therapy[^3]. Although the complete quantitative dataset was not available in the current extract, this study is the first dedicated multicentre evaluation of above-label eltrombopag dosing and addresses a recognised real-world clinical practice where some haematologists empirically dose-escalate before switching agents. The findings are expected to inform whether a formal high-dose escalation pathway should precede transition to alternative agents such as avatrombopag or romiplostim in patients with inadequate response.

**Proteomics Biomarkers to Differentiate ITP from MYH9-RD.** A *BJH* study by Luo et al. employs data-independent acquisition plasma proteomics to distinguish MYH9-related disease (MYH9-RD) from ITP — two causes of macrothrombocytopenia that are often clinically indistinguishable without genetic confirmation[^6]. Profiling cohorts of MYH9-RD (n = 10), ITP (n = 10), and healthy controls (n = 14) quantified 2,533 proteins, identifying 91 MYH9-RD–specific and 124 ITP-specific proteins. Machine-learning algorithms (Boruta and Random Forest) derived candidate biomarker panels: for MYH9-RD, von Willebrand factor (VWF), cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP), apolipoprotein A-1 (APOA1), and periostin (POSTN); for ITP, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and carboxypeptidase B2 (CPB2). These panels demonstrated excellent apparent discrimination within the discovery cohort, though rigorous external validation is explicitly acknowledged as required before clinical application. For centres without rapid access to MYH9 genetic testing, this hypothesis-generating framework suggests that plasma proteomic signatures may eventually serve as a practical triage tool to reduce misdiagnosis and unnecessary immunosuppression in MYH9-RD patients who would otherwise receive ITP-directed therapy.

**Genetic Screening in ITP.** A *Haematologica* study by Di Lieto et al. reports that systematic genetic screening alters both the final diagnosis and subsequent clinical management in a proportion of patients presenting with apparent ITP, reinforcing the view that inherited thrombocytopenia is underrecognised in the ITP diagnostic workup[^7]. Detailed quantitative data were not available in the current extract, but this work adds further support for low-threshold genetic panel testing — particularly in patients with a family history, atypical bleeding phenotype, or absent response to standard ITP therapies — a consideration of direct relevance given the availability of next-generation sequencing panels at NCKUH.

**Emergency Splenectomy in ITP with Intracranial Haemorrhage.** A *Haematologica* case series by Prakash et al. addresses the high-stakes scenario of ITP presenting with intracranial haemorrhage, evaluating the role and outcomes of emergency splenectomy in this setting[^8]. Detailed outcome data were not available in the current extract, but this publication provides contemporary case-level evidence for a procedure that remains clinically relevant in resource-limited or rapidly deteriorating presentations where pharmacological bridging has failed.

## II. TTP / HUS / Thrombotic Microangiopathy

A *Haematologica* editorial by De Cristofaro examines the mechanistic intersection of CAR-T cell therapy toxicity and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), reviewing the endothelial injury cascade initiated by cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell–associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis as drivers of transplant-associated TMA — a complication of increasing clinical relevance as CAR-T utilisation expands in both malignant and autoimmune indications[^9]. The editorial contextualises endothelial injury as the common effector pathway linking immune activation to complement dysregulation and microvascular thrombosis, and discusses the emerging evidence for complement-directed intervention in this setting. For haematologists managing TMA in the post-CAR-T context at NCKUH, this review reinforces the importance of early TMA screening protocols in patients receiving cellular therapies.

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

Two mechanistically significant studies published this week advance understanding of haemostatic biology in haemophilia A, with direct implications for therapeutic strategy.

**Factor V as an Endogenous Anticoagulant in Haemophilia A.** A *Blood* study by Jewell et al. challenges the canonical view that procofactor FV functions solely as a procoagulant cofactor and demonstrates, through a series of carefully designed plasma experiments and purified-component assays, that FV exerts a previously underappreciated anticoagulant function by limiting FX activation by the tissue factor:FVIIa (TF:FVIIa) complex[^5]. In TF-initiated coagulation experiments conducted in FV/FVIII-immunodepleted plasma reconstituted with titrated FV concentrations in the absence of tissue factor pathway inhibitor α (TFPIα), thrombin generation peaked at 2 nM FV and fell progressively as FV concentration increased; at the physiological plasma concentration of approximately 20 nM FV, peak thrombin and thrombin generation rate were reduced by 50–80%, with the inhibitory effect amplified at low FVIII concentrations. In purified TF:FVIIa assays, FV reduced FX activation by approximately 80% at physiological concentration, and this inhibition was partially relieved by blocking the FV light chain or C2 domain, implicating specific FV structural domains in membrane-dependent FX competition. In haemophilia A plasma, higher FV was independently associated with prolonged lag time and time-to-peak thrombin generation, confirming the physiological relevance of this anticoagulant mechanism in the haemophilic milieu. These findings refine coagulation initiation models and may help explain why patients with identical FVIII deficiency severity display variable bleeding phenotypes — natural FV variation may serve as a modifying coagulant brake.

**Therapeutic Advances in Haemophilia A and Von Willebrand Disease — Comparative Review.** A *Haematologica* perspective by Seidizadeh, Peyvandi, and Mannucci provides a structured comparison of the parallel — but asymmetric — therapeutic evolution in haemophilia A (HA) and von Willebrand disease (VWD)[^13]. In HA, the trajectory from plasma-derived concentrates through recombinant FVIII, extended half-life products, and non-factor therapies (emicizumab as FVIII mimetic) to the cusp of gene therapy has established prophylaxis as the universal standard of care. In VWD, the therapeutic arc has been slower, attributable to the marked molecular and clinical heterogeneity of VWD (types 1, 2A, 2B, 2M, 2N, and 3), persistent diagnostic delays, and the relative scarcity of VWF-directed clinical trial infrastructure. Current VWD management rests on desmopressin for type 1 and some type 2 variants, plasma-derived VWF concentrates, and recombinant VWF (vonicog alfa), with limited systematic implementation of long-term prophylaxis. The authors highlight an expanding pipeline for VWD encompassing VWF half-life extension strategies, VWF-independent coagulation rebalancing agents (fitusiran, marstacimab, concizumab), platelet-mimetic technologies, antifibrinolytics, and gene-based approaches, predicting an imminent narrowing of the therapeutic gap between VWD and HA.

**Blood-Induced Joint Disease in Haemophilia — Mechanisms and Novel Targets.** A *Haematologica* review by Valentino and Dargaud provides a detailed synthesis of the molecular pathogenesis of haemophilic arthropathy and identifies actionable therapeutic targets beyond haemostatic correction[^14]. The review describes how iron deposition in the synovium following haemarthrosis activates oncogene-like programmes in synovial fibroblasts (including c-MYC and MDM2), promotes angiogenesis, and drives villous hypertrophy through persistent oxidative stress and IL-1β/TNF-α signalling. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses reveal endothelial cells undergoing ferroptosis signatures and increased permeability with FTL→SCARA5-fibroblast iron-responsive signalling circuits that may predispose to re-bleeding. Even brief exposures to low blood concentrations are sufficient to induce irreversible chondrocyte apoptosis, reduced proteoglycan synthesis, and ROS-driven matrix degradation. The authors propose a mechanistic treatment framework targeting iron/ROS modulation, endothelial ferroptosis inhibition, mast-cell stabilisation, TNF-α pathway blockade via iRhom2/ADAM17, fibroblast state reprogramming, and activation of TNXB–AKT cartilage survival pathways. This review is directly relevant to managing the substantial subset of NCKUH haemophilia patients who have already accrued significant joint damage despite adequate FVIII/FIX prophylaxis coverage.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis)

**Sickle Cell Disease — Sub-Phenotype Classification.** A large *AJH* latent class analysis by Willen et al. (n = 7,636 SCD patients, California administrative databases 1991–2019) formally validates the existence of clinically distinct complication-based sub-phenotypes in SCD using an unsupervised statistical approach[^15]. Latent class analysis stratified by sex identified four classes in both males and females: a Vaso-Occlusive class (high VOE, acute chest syndrome, avascular necrosis; 23% of males, 15% of females), a Hemolytic class (high chronic kidney disease, pulmonary hypertension, stroke; 8% of males, 13% of females), an Overlap class (8% of each sex), and a Low Complications class (62–63%). In the younger sub-cohort (≤25 years; n = 2,210), class structure was modified: a distinct ACS-dominant class emerged (12%), while the Hemolytic class had the worst survival in youth (HR 7.8, p < 0.001). Across all ages, Vaso-Occlusive, Hemolytic, and Overlap classes had elevated mortality compared with the Low Complications class (HR 1.3–2.4, p < 0.05 for all comparisons). Importantly, these sub-phenotypes are clinically actionable — patients in the Hemolytic class would be expected to derive greater benefit from interventions targeting haemolysis and end-organ sequelae (hydroxyurea, voxelotor, L-glutamine) rather than purely anti-sickling strategies, while Vaso-Occlusive class patients represent the target population for crizanlizumab and aggressive pain management protocols.

**Sickle Cell Disease — LPA Signalling as Target for Chronic Pain.** A *Blood* experimental study by Viatchenko-Karpinski et al. identifies lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) signalling through LPA receptor 1 (LPA1R) as a previously unrecognised driver of chronic pain in SCD, using humanised transgenic HbSS Berkeley mice[^16]. HbSS mice exhibited elevated plasma LPA levels and upregulated LPA1R mRNA in L1–L5 dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons. Blocking LPA synthesis with BI-2545, or silencing/antagonising LPA1R with siRNA or the antagonist AM966, reversed both mechanical and heat hyperalgesia in HbSS mice. At the electrophysiological level, nociceptive DRG neurons in hyperalgesic HbSS mice demonstrated lower rheobase, more depolarised resting membrane potential, and higher action potential frequency — parameters all normalised by LPA1R siRNA pretreatment — with reduced outward potassium channel currents identified as the primary sensitisation mechanism. Given that opioids remain the predominant treatment for SCD-related chronic pain despite their substantial morbidity, LPA1R antagonism represents a mechanistically novel, non-opioid analgesic strategy warranting early-phase clinical investigation.

**Sickle Cell Disease — Clonal Haematopoiesis After HSCT.** A *Blood* study by Weeks et al. (n = 98 SCD recipients, n = 72 non-SCD donor controls, error-corrected DNA sequencing, variant allele fraction threshold >0.0005) characterises the landscape of clonal haematopoiesis (CH) in the context of nonmyeloablative HSCT for SCD[^17]. Baseline CH prevalence was similar in SCD recipients and donors; however, SCD recipients had 3.8-fold higher odds of carrying DNA damage response (DDR)-mutant CH involving TP53, PPM1D, ATM, and CHEK2 mutations compared with non-SCD donors, consistent with the chronic genotoxic stress of haemolysis and vascular inflammation in SCD. Following HCT, the proportion of SCD recipients with CH increased, driven by DNMT3A/TET2 mutations in engrafted donor-derived CH and by TP53-mutant persistent-recipient CH. Three cases of fatal TP53-mutant MDS/AML were observed. These findings have direct implications for pre-HSCT screening and post-HSCT surveillance protocols in SCD gene therapy and allogeneic transplant programmes.

**Dengue-Associated Thrombocytopenia — Immature Platelet Fraction.** A *BJH* retrospective longitudinal study by Singh et al. (n = 667 dengue patients) demonstrates that Day 1 immature platelet fraction (IPF) independently predicts platelet recovery trajectory in dengue-associated thrombocytopenia[^18]. Median platelet counts declined from 118 to 98 × 10⁹/L between Days 1 and 5 (p = 0.0017), while IPF simultaneously increased from 9.7% to 12.9% (p < 0.0001). Critically, a higher baseline IPF predicted significantly faster platelet recovery (p < 0.0001), and the proportion of patients with continued platelet decline at Day 5 was substantially lower in the high-IPF group than the low-IPF group (28.6% vs. 47.8%; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.013). These data validate IPF as a dynamic biomarker of compensatory thrombopoiesis that can be used to risk-stratify dengue patients for platelet transfusion need, a particularly relevant finding in Southeast Asia including Taiwan where dengue-related thrombocytopenia drives high haematology consultation volumes and platelet resource utilisation at NCKUH each dengue season.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

A comprehensive *BJH* review by Zhang, Nemeth, and Sangkhae addresses maternal iron homeostasis, disorders, and pregnancy outcomes, synthesising evidence from physiological, biomarker, and therapeutic domains[^19]. Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) affects 12–80% of pregnant women globally (prevalence varying by region) and constitutes the leading cause of years lived with disability in women of reproductive age worldwide. The review critically appraises the limitations of conventional iron biomarkers during pregnancy (ferritin is an acute-phase reactant; transferrin saturation fluctuates with haemodilution), advocates for early and repeated screening throughout gestation rather than relying on haemoglobin as the primary diagnostic trigger, and evaluates the evidence for oral versus intravenous iron therapy. Iron overload in pregnancy — while far less prevalent — is also associated with adverse placental and fetal outcomes through mechanisms that remain incompletely characterised, and the authors call for dedicated mechanistic and clinical studies in this underrecognised condition. For Taiwanese practitioners, this review is timely given the high prevalence of concurrent thalassaemia trait and iron deficiency in the obstetric population, where appropriate differentiation between trait-related microcytosis and true IDA remains a frequent diagnostic challenge.

**Iron Homeostasis — RNF217 and Ferroportin Regulation in Hereditary Haemochromatosis.** A *Blood* study by Yu et al. demonstrates that overexpression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF217 — whether global or intestine-specific — rescues the iron-loading phenotype in hemojuvelin knockout (Hjv−/−) mice, a model of juvenile haemochromatosis, by directly modulating intestinal ferroportin protein levels independently of hepcidin[^20]. Intestine-specific Rnf217 overexpression significantly reduced iron accumulation in serum and vital organs without changes in hepcidin levels, identifying duodenal enterocytes as the primary site of RNF217-mediated iron absorption control and establishing a hepcidin-bypass therapeutic mechanism. This work suggests that intestinal RNF217 modulation may represent a viable therapeutic strategy for hepcidin-resistant or hepcidin-deficient iron-overload disorders, including specific subtypes of hereditary haemochromatosis where hepcidin augmentation strategies (minihepcidins, TMPRSS6 inhibition) are less effective.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

**Rare Inherited Coagulation Disorders and Thrombotic Risk — Paradigm Review.** A *Haematologica* review by Franchini, Mohsenian, and Mannucci synthesises data from international registries, clinical trials, and biochemical studies to map the thrombotic risk landscape across rare inherited coagulation disorders (RICDs)[^4]. The traditional clinical assumption — that reduced coagulation factor activity uniformly increases bleeding risk — is shown to be inadequate. FXII deficiency and FXI deficiency, for example, appear paradoxically protective against thrombosis: FXII participates in contact-phase activation of pathological thrombus formation in arterial beds, and FXI inhibition is being actively investigated as an anticoagulant strategy. In contrast, select molecular variants in prothrombin disorders (dysprothrombinemias), fibrinogen disorders (dysfibrinogenemias with certain mutations), FXIII deficiency, and afibrinogenemia are associated with genuine thrombotic risk through distinct mechanisms: dysprothrombinemia impairs anticoagulant regulatory mechanisms leading to excess thrombin generation; FXIII deficiency causes mechanical clot instability and loss of thrombin sequestration; and in afibrinogenemia, paradoxical arterial thrombosis has been reported. The authors emphasise that thrombotic risk in these patients is frequently triggered or amplified by replacement therapies, co-inherited thrombophilia, or acquired haemostatic perturbations — reinforcing the need for individualised risk assessment rather than applying uniform factor-level–to–bleeding-risk algorithms.

**Hereditary Antithrombin Deficiency in Pregnancy.** A *BJH* "in a nutshell" review by Afolabi, Ruder, and DeSancho characterises hereditary antithrombin (AT) deficiency as the highest-risk inherited thrombophilia in pregnancy and provides a practical management framework[^2]. Pregnancy markedly amplifies thrombotic risk in AT deficiency due to the physiological reduction in AT levels across gestation and the profound hypercoagulable state of the third trimester and postpartum period. The review endorses individualised anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), with AT concentrate replacement reserved for high-risk situations including peripartum management, surgery, or when LMWH anticoagulation is inadequate or contraindicated. The review highlights that anti-Xa monitoring is essential for LMWH dose optimisation in AT deficiency (where AT is required for LMWH's anticoagulant mechanism), and that target anti-Xa levels should be maintained at the higher end of therapeutic range. Multidisciplinary haematology–maternal-fetal medicine collaboration is consistently identified as a determinant of maternal and fetal survival. For haematologists at NCKUH, AT deficiency in pregnancy is uncommon but disproportionately morbid, and this review provides actionable guidance for when to initiate AT concentrate infusion, particularly peripartum.

**Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia After Aortic Dissection Repair.** A *Haematologica* case report by Blankstein et al. describes an unusual clinical scenario of acute heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) following aortic dissection repair, in which platelet count recovery and seroreversion of anti-platelet factor 4/heparin antibodies occurred despite ongoing device-related heparin exposure[^21]. This case challenges the conventional expectation that any ongoing heparin exposure will perpetuate HIT and illustrates the clinical heterogeneity of antibody-mediated HIT resolution — relevant for cardiac surgery haematology consultation where device circuits (e.g., ventricular assist devices, ECMO) may require ongoing systemic heparinisation in the context of recent HIT.

**Recombinant Human Thrombopoietin in Chronic Liver Disease–Associated Thrombocytopenia.** A notable Phase III trial result published this week in *AJH* by Han et al. deserves dedicated attention as it addresses a gap in clinical practice in Asia where recombinant human thrombopoietin (rhTPO) has NHI-type registration pathways that differ from Western markets[^23]. In this multicentre, randomised (2:1), double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase III trial (n = 120 adult Chinese patients with chronic liver disease [CLD] and platelet count <50 × 10⁹/L), rhTPO was administered once daily for up to 5–7 days prior to elective invasive procedures. The primary endpoint — proportion of patients maintaining sustained platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L from 24 hours before the procedure to 7 days post-procedure without emergency bleeding management — was achieved by 85.0% of rhTPO-treated patients versus 12.5% of placebo-treated patients (p < 0.0001). Preoperative platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L was achieved in 92.5% versus 20.0% (p < 0.0001). Platelet transfusion was avoided in 92.5% of rhTPO-treated patients versus 25.0% of placebo (p < 0.0001), and the median duration of platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L was substantially longer with rhTPO (21.0 vs. 3.0 days, p = 0.0007). Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 12.5% of both groups, with no treatment-related serious adverse events. In the context of the approved thrombopoietin receptor agonist avatrombopag (which is indicated for CLD-associated thrombocytopenia in Western markets), the rhTPO data provide a regionally relevant alternative, particularly for centres in Taiwan and broader East Asia where rhTPO (which acts by directly stimulating the endogenous TPO receptor as an exogenous TPO protein rather than a small-molecule agonist) is prescribed and may have logistical or cost advantages.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings this week carry specific relevance for haematology practice in Taiwan.

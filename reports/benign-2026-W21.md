## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a phase 2 trial of bomedemstat — an LSD1/KDM1A inhibitor — in 73 patients with relapsed/refractory essential thrombocythemia demonstrates a 77% platelet response rate at Week 24 and, critically, a variant allele frequency decrease in driver mutations (CALR, JAK2, or MPL) in 85% of evaluable patients, suggesting disease-modifying potential beyond cytoreduction[^1]. Second, the TOWAR phase 3 cluster-randomised trial (NEJM) of 1,020 trauma patients requiring prehospital transfusion finds that type-O whole blood does not reduce 30-day mortality compared with blood components (30-day mortality 25.9% vs. 20.5%; adjusted OR 1.24; 95% CI 0.87–1.76; p = 0.24), challenging prevailing enthusiasm for universal-donor whole-blood programmes in pre-hospital trauma[^2]. Third, a multiomics analysis in *Blood Advances* identifies ferroptosis activation, autophagy suppression, and cytoskeletal fragility as the molecular determinants distinguishing transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) from non-TDT among patients with identical compound heterozygous globin mutations, reframing disease severity as a post-transcriptional phenomenon beyond genotype[^3]. Fourth, a *Blood Advances* proteomics study of 376 SCD patients identifies a 499-protein plasma mortality signature — anchored in IL-18 signalling — with an adjusted HR of 2.2 (95% CI 1.3–3.6; p = 0.0032) for SCD death and demonstrates that SCD patients are biologically approximately 6 years older than their chronological age by plasma proteome estimation[^4]. Fifth, a *Blood Advances* population genetics study of 469,589 UK Biobank participants demonstrates that digenic and multigenic heterozygous FHL (familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis) genotypes — present in ~0.1% of the general population — confer no increased risk for HLH-associated phenotypes (OR 0.95; p = 1), systematically refuting the "pathway defect accumulation" model of HLH susceptibility[^5].

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

A *Blood Advances* multiomics study by Mitra et al. provides the most granular molecular characterisation to date of phenotypic severity divergence in thalassemia patients carrying identical compound heterozygous mutations (IVS 1-5 G>C and CD 26 G>A, i.e., the HbE/β-thalassemia compound heterozygote), drawn from a cohort of 285 hemoglobinopathy patients[^3]. Five patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) were compared against five with non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia (NTDT) using RNA sequencing (NovaSeq) and proteomics (Orbitrap mass spectrometry) on isolated red blood cells. The integrated transcriptome-proteome analysis revealed a global loss of mRNA-protein concordance in TDT, with CDK11A and MCTS1 losing positive correlation, while RLP38 and H3C1 showed compensatory overtranslation linked to erythropoiesis-regulating transcription factors. In TDT specifically, four proteins — WNK3, HNRNPUL1, COPS7A, and HTATSF1 — demonstrated discordant expression indicating posttranscriptional aberrations. Protein-to-transcript ratio analysis documented reduced cytoskeletal expression (ankyrin and spectrin), elevated chaperone activity, increased ferroptosis markers (ferritin heavy chain 1, ferritin light chain, heme oxygenase-1), and suppressed autophagy as the defining molecular fingerprint of the more severe TDT phenotype. These findings conceptually reframe the clinical observation that two patients with identical globin genotypes can have dramatically different transfusion requirements: the difference appears to reside in post-transcriptional splicing dysfunction, ferroptosis activation, and cytoskeletal fragility rather than in the primary mutation itself. For Taiwan — where HbE-containing thalassemia compound heterozygotes are encountered in the Southeast Asian immigrant and aboriginal populations, and where β-thalassemia carrier frequency reaches approximately 1–2% — these data reinforce the need for proteomics-informed severity stratification beyond standard HPLC genotyping when counselling transfusion-threshold decisions.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

**Prehospital Whole Blood vs. Component Therapy in Trauma Hemorrhage — TOWAR Trial**

The TOWAR trial — a pragmatic, multicenter, phase 3, cluster-randomised trial — enrolled 1,020 eligible trauma patients transported by 44 air medical bases randomly assigned 2:1 to up to 2 units of type-O whole blood or standard blood components (plasma, red cells, or both) during prehospital resuscitation[^2]. In the primary analysis (695 whole-blood vs. 298 component-therapy patients), 30-day all-cause mortality was 25.9% in the whole-blood group compared with 20.5% in the component group (adjusted OR 1.24; 95% CI 0.87–1.76; p = 0.24), with no significant between-group difference. An observational sub-study further demonstrated that whole-blood storage age did not materially affect outcomes: 30-day mortality was 27.1% among 210 patients receiving whole blood stored 15–21 days versus 26.4% among 443 patients receiving blood stored 1–14 days (adjusted OR 0.99; 95% CI 0.74–1.32). No substantial differences in adverse events were observed between groups. These results are clinically important for haematologists involved in transfusion medicine and damage-control resuscitation protocols: they demonstrate that the theoretical haemostatic advantages of whole blood — restoration of near-physiological red cell, plasma, and platelet ratios in a single product — did not translate into a mortality benefit over standard component therapy in the prehospital setting. The trial was adequately powered and used a cluster-randomised design appropriate for prehospital logistics, lending its null result considerable credibility. Hospitals and blood banks in Taiwan considering whole-blood programmes for trauma indications should weigh this evidence alongside logistical and blood safety considerations.

**Plasma Proteome Mortality Signatures in Sickle Cell Disease**

While primarily a sickle cell disease study, this *Blood Advances* proteomics investigation by Chignon et al. has direct implications for understanding coagulation- and inflammation-driven vascular mortality[^4]. Among 376 SCD patients, plasma mortality risk scores developed in non-SCD populations were significantly elevated in SCD (p = 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁰), and the composite score was independently associated with SCD mortality after adjustment for standard risk factors (HR 2.2; 95% CI 1.3–3.6; p = 0.0032). The 499 SCD-mortality-associated proteins were enriched for inflammatory pathways, prominently the IL-18 signalling cascade. Biological age estimated from the proteome exceeded chronological age by a mean of 6.0 ± 5.4 years in SCD patients, with brain biological age positively correlating with prior stroke occurrence. These data support the hypothesis that chronic vasculopathy and coagulation-inflammation crosstalk — rather than acute vaso-occlusion alone — drive long-term mortality in SCD, and suggest that IL-18 pathway inhibition may warrant prospective investigation as a disease-modifying strategy.

**Digenic and Multigenic FHL Variants — Population Refutation of the Accumulation Model**

A *Blood Advances* population genetics study by Borisov et al. evaluated 469,589 UK Biobank participants and a German FHL cohort for digenic and multigenic heterozygous variants in FHL genes (PRF1, UNC13D, STX11, STXBP2, RAB27A, and LYST)[^5]. Digenic FHL genotypes were identified in 575 UK Biobank individuals (0.1% prevalence), without enrichment for HLH-associated phenotypes (OR 0.95; p = 1). Trigenic carriers numbered four; none had HLH-related diagnoses. When the common low-penetrance variants PRF1 p.Ala91Val and p.Asn252Ser were included, the digenic carrier number expanded to 2,590, but again without phenotype enrichment. In the German FHL cohort, no symptomatic patient with abnormal NK/CTL degranulation carried digenic or multigenic heterozygous variants. Furthermore, several HGMD-labelled "pathogenic" FHL variants were observed biallelically in asymptomatic UK Biobank adults, raising significant concerns about variant misclassification in this gene set. These findings have immediate clinical and genetic counselling implications: haematologists and clinical geneticists should not classify digenic heterozygous FHL carriers as at-risk for HLH, and genetic reports citing multigenic heterozygosity as an explanation for HLH susceptibility should be interpreted with caution pending functional NK/CTL degranulation assays.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings this week carry direct relevance to clinical practice at NCKUH and affiliated centres in Taiwan. The thalassemia multiomics study[^3] is particularly pertinent given Taiwan's estimated β-thalassemia carrier rate of approximately 1–2% and an alpha-thalassemia carrier rate of 4–5%, with HbH disease and HbE-related compound heterozygotes increasingly encountered in the context of immigration from Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines). The molecular distinctions identified between TDT and NTDT in genotype-matched patients suggest that future NHI-funded thalassemia screening programmes might benefit from proteomics or transcriptomics enrichment to stratify transfusion thresholds beyond HPLC haemoglobin quantitation.

The TOWAR trial results[^2] are relevant to Taiwan's trauma transfusion infrastructure: several medical centres (NTUH, CGMH, Tri-Service General Hospital) have been evaluating or implementing walking-blood-bank or low-titre group-O whole-blood protocols following global military medicine experience. The null mortality result from TOWAR should prompt evidence-based re-evaluation of these programmes, particularly in the absence of Taiwan-specific data on prehospital whole-blood logistics.

The bomedemstat ET data[^1] are relevant given that ET is a common MPN in Taiwan's NHI haematology practice. Bomedemstat is not currently NHI-reimbursed in Taiwan; cytoreductive standard of care remains hydroxyurea and anagrelide. The 85% driver mutation VAF reduction rate reported in this phase 2 trial may support future NHI evaluation if phase 3 data confirm disease modification.

The FHL population genetics study[^5] has implications for Taiwan's emerging clinical genomics infrastructure: as multi-gene panel testing for unexplained cytopenias expands in academic centres (NCKUH, NTUH), clinicians should avoid over-interpreting digenic heterozygous FHL variants without confirmatory NK/CTL degranulation functional testing.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The most immediately practice-changing finding this week is the TOWAR trial, which delivers a definitive negative result for prehospital type-O whole blood over component therapy in trauma haemorrhage, directly challenging a strategy that has gained considerable traction globally. Haematologists engaged in transfusion medicine programme development should incorporate this evidence before committing institutional resources to whole-blood procurement and logistics.

The bomedemstat phase 2 data represent the most compelling new pharmacological signal in non-malignant haematology this week: an 85% driver mutation VAF reduction rate in ET at 24 weeks would, if durable, constitute genuine disease modification — a therapeutic frontier not achieved by hydroxyurea or anagrelide. The phase 3 programme should be watched closely.

The thalassemia multiomics findings challenge the long-held clinical axiom that genotype determines transfusion dependence, opening a path toward proteomics-guided prognostication. The FHL population genetics study provides the strongest evidence to date against the clinical use of digenic carrier status as an HLH risk determinant. Together, these two genomic/proteomic studies underscore the need for functional validation of variant pathogenicity across inherited haematological disorders.

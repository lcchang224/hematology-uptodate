## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a *Blood* "How I Treat" review by Leaf and Sykes provides the first comprehensive clinical framework for managing autoimmune neutropenia (AiN) in adults, characterising this as a predominantly chronic, female-predominant disorder in which anti-neutrophil antibodies are detectable in only approximately 50% of cases, and systematically reviewing evidence for observation, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, immunosuppression, and emerging plasma cell–directed and complement-blocking strategies[^1]. Second, a prespecified subgroup analysis of the AZALEA-TIMI 71 trial published in *Blood* demonstrates that the factor XI (FXI) inhibitor abelacimab reduces bleeding across all strata of prior oral anticoagulant (OAC) experience in atrial fibrillation, with potentially greater absolute benefit in OAC-naïve patients — extending the safety dataset for this mechanistically novel anticoagulant class[^2]. Third, a phase III multicentre randomised controlled trial published in *AJH* demonstrates that recombinant human thrombopoietin (rhTPO) achieves the primary endpoint of sustained platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L without emergency bleeding management in 85.0% versus 12.5% of placebo-treated patients with chronic liver disease–related thrombocytopenia undergoing invasive procedures (p <0.0001), while avoiding platelet transfusion in 92.5% of treated patients[^3]. Fourth, a *Blood* mechanistic study by Duan et al. identifies l-carnitine–driven fatty acid oxidation as a critical metabolic regulator of human haematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) regeneration, demonstrating functional rescue of CD34⁺ HSPCs from aplastic anaemia patients ex vivo and in vivo — providing a potential metabolic therapeutic strategy for marrow failure syndromes[^4]. Fifth, a *BJH* proteomics study by Luo et al. identifies distinct plasma protein signatures distinguishing MYH9-related disease (MYH9-RD) from immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) using data-independent acquisition proteomics, proposing preliminary biomarker panels with potential to resolve this clinically challenging differential diagnosis[^5].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

**Proteomics-Based Differential Diagnosis of MYH9-RD versus ITP.** The clinical differentiation of MYH9-related disease from immune thrombocytopenia remains a recognised diagnostic challenge, particularly in paediatric patients presenting with isolated macrothrombocytopenia before genetic testing results are available. Luo et al. published in *BJH* a hypothesis-generating plasma proteomics study using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry in cohorts of MYH9-RD (n = 10), ITP (n = 10), and healthy controls (n = 14), quantifying 2,533 proteins across groups[^5]. The study identified 91 MYH9-RD–specific and 124 ITP-specific proteins, with the two diseases exhibiting fundamentally divergent immune and metabolic remodelling signatures. Using protein–protein interaction network analysis integrated with Boruta and Random Forest machine-learning algorithms, the authors identified candidate biomarker panels: for MYH9-RD, the panel comprised von Willebrand factor (VWF), cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP), apolipoprotein A-1 (APOA1), and periostin (POSTN); for ITP, the panel comprised neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and carboxypeptidase B2 (CPB2). Although the small cohort size and absence of external validation limit direct clinical applicability at this time, the biological plausibility is compelling — the MYH9-RD panel reflects cytoskeletal and platelet structural biology, while the ITP panel reflects immune-mediated fibrinolytic dysregulation. For practitioners at NCKUH encountering paediatric macrothrombocytopenia of unclear aetiology, this study provides both a mechanistic framework and a preliminary basis for prospective biomarker validation initiatives.

Separately, a conceptual review by Wu, Tomei, and Ni published in *BJH* revisits the expanding biology of platelets beyond haemostasis, emphasising their roles in inflammation and adaptive immunity as directly relevant to immune-mediated thrombocytopenia[^6]. While primarily a narrative synthesis rather than new clinical trial data, it contextualises immune-mediated platelet destruction within the broader biology of platelet–immune cell crosstalk, reinforcing mechanistic rationale for immunomodulatory therapeutic approaches.

## III. Aplastic Anemia

**L-Carnitine as a Metabolic Regulator of HSPC Regeneration.** Duan et al. published in *Blood* a study applying low-input mass spectrometry–based metabolomics to 13 immunophenotypically defined haematopoietic cell populations from adult human bone marrow, detecting more than 80 metabolites with as few as approximately 10,000 cells per sample[^4]. The principal finding was the identification of l-carnitine–driven fatty acid oxidation as a key metabolic feature of primitive HSPCs. Mechanistically, l-carnitine was shown to activate the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha–transcription factor EB (PPARα–TFEB) signalling axis, promoting mitochondrial metabolism and autophagy to preserve regenerative capacity. Critically for aplastic anaemia, functional assays in primary CD34⁺ HSPCs derived from aplastic anaemia patients confirmed that l-carnitine supplementation improved stem cell function both ex vivo and in vivo in xenograft models, with rescue of erythroid colony formation observed alongside improved engraftment. A companion perspective published concurrently in *Blood* by Filippi frames this work as a platform for understanding metabolic vulnerabilities in HSPC biology[^7]. Although these findings are currently preclinical, they identify a potentially accessible, low-toxicity metabolic intervention that could complement existing immunosuppressive strategies in aplastic anaemia. A formal phase I/II evaluation of l-carnitine supplementation in aplastic anaemia is the logical next step, and NCKUH haematologists with access to aplastic anaemia clinical trial infrastructure may wish to consider investigator-initiated protocols.

**Romiplostim plus Immunosuppression in Aplastic Anaemia: 2-Year Interim Analysis.** Lee, Hosokawa, Jang, Obara et al. published in *BJH* a 2-year interim analysis of an observational study pooling data from two clinical trials of romiplostim combined with immunosuppressive therapy (IST) as first-line treatment in aplastic anaemia[^8]. The full quantitative results are not yet available in the current extract; however, this represents an important longitudinal data point for the combination of thrombopoietin receptor agonism with IST, which has been evaluated previously in shorter follow-up windows. The 2-year timepoint is clinically significant because it captures late responses, relapse rates, clonal evolution, and sustained blood count independence — the key endpoints that distinguish durable haematological recovery from transient cytokine-mediated responses. Practitioners managing aplastic anaemia patients on romiplostim-based IST regimens should note this update as providing the longest prospective safety and efficacy data for this combination to date. Full quantitative data are anticipated in the final publication.

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

**BSH/UKHCDO Good Practice Paper: Multidisciplinary Haematology–Gynaecology Service.** A Good Practice Paper co-published by the British Society for Haematology and United Kingdom Haemophilia Centre Doctors' Organisation in *BJH* provides structured guidance on implementing and sustaining multidisciplinary haematology–gynaecology services for individuals with bleeding and thrombotic disorders[^9]. While the document focuses on the UK healthcare setting, its recommendations are directly applicable to the design of joint haematology–obstetrics/gynaecology services in tertiary academic centres such as NCKUH, where women with inherited bleeding disorders (including von Willebrand disease, haemophilia carriers, and platelet function disorders) require integrated menstrual and reproductive management. The guidance outlines minimum service components, referral pathways, and clinical audit metrics. For haematologists at NCKUH managing female patients with bleeding disorders across their reproductive lifespan, this paper provides a structured framework for service development and quality benchmarking.

## VI. Von Willebrand Disease

**Bevacizumab for Acquired Gastrointestinal Vascular Malformations Including VWF Deficiency.** Ayad et al. published in *AJH* an observational cohort study evaluating systemic bevacizumab — an anti-VEGF-A monoclonal antibody — in 32 patients (median age 75 years, 44% female) with acquired gastrointestinal vascular malformations causing severe anaemia, including patients with von Willebrand factor deficiency[^10]. The primary outcome measure was the composite Hematologic Support Score (HSS), integrating red cell units transfused and elemental iron infused. The median HSS improved from 15.04 (95% CI 11.16–21.80) red cell unit equivalents in the 6 months before treatment to 4.08 (95% CI 4.00–8.00) during months 1–6 of bevacizumab (p <0.001) and further to 0.00 (95% CI 0.00–5.40) during months 7–12 (p <0.001). Units of red cells transfused and quantity of elemental iron infused both improved significantly as independent endpoints. Median haemoglobin rose by 3.1 g/dL after bevacizumab initiation (p <0.001), and the annualised hospitalisation/emergency department visit rate decreased from 3 to 1 event per year (p = 0.01). Bevacizumab was generally tolerated; proteinuria and hypertension occurred in 25% and 16% of patients respectively, leading to discontinuation in 9%. This study is the first to report bevacizumab on a predefined institutional pathway in this population and is notable for including patients with VWF deficiency — a subset for whom no disease-specific vascular lesion therapy previously existed. For NCKUH haematologists managing patients with type 2A or acquired von Willebrand syndrome complicated by recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding unresponsive to haemostatic therapy, bevacizumab represents an emerging option deserving prospective investigation.

## VIII. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

**Autoantibodies to Band 3 Epitopes in *Plasmodium vivax*–Associated Anemia.** Marzano-Miranda et al. published in *AJH* a study examining autoantibody responses against extracellular loop epitopes of Band 3 (a major erythrocyte transmembrane protein) in patients with *Plasmodium vivax* infection[^11]. Among 79 anemic and 95 non-anemic infected patients (with 40 healthy non-endemic controls), anemic individuals exhibited more prominent autoantibody responses, particularly against loops 4 and 6. IgG3 was the predominant immunoglobulin subclass targeting these loops and remained detectable for up to 28 days post-treatment, suggesting a mechanistic contribution to post-treatment anaemia persistence even after parasite clearance. IgG1 was the principal subclass recognising loops 1, 2, and 3. While this study addresses a geographically specific aetiology — *P. vivax* malaria — it is mechanistically relevant to haematologists managing travel-related or endemic tropical anaemia, and reinforces the concept that uninfected red cell destruction via autoimmune mechanisms (resembling warm AIHA) contributes substantially to malaria-associated anaemia beyond direct parasitisation. In Taiwan, where *P. vivax* is non-endemic but imported cases are seen in returning travellers, this study is clinically informative for the differential diagnosis of Coombs-positive haemolytic anaemia in the appropriate epidemiological context.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis)

**BPNT1 Loss-of-Function and Vitamin B12–Dependent Megaloblastic Anemia.** Zeng et al. published in *Blood* a report identifying biallelic loss-of-function mutations in *BPNT1* in three patients with recurrent vitamin B12–dependent megaloblastic anaemia[^12]. Mechanistically, BPNT1 deficiency was shown to cause accumulation of 3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphate (PAP), impaired ribosome biogenesis, and reduced ileal expression of the cubilin/amnionless receptor complex in *Bpnt1*-null mice — the latter providing a molecular explanation for impaired cobalamin absorption. This finding is important because it defines a new genetic aetiology of megaloblastic anaemia that is phenotypically indistinguishable from nutritional B12 deficiency at presentation but requires genetic diagnosis to confirm. Patients with recurrent or unexplained B12-responsive anaemia, particularly in the paediatric or young adult setting, should be considered for targeted or whole-exome sequencing to evaluate *BPNT1* variants. The cubilin/amnionless pathway disruption also distinguishes this from intrinsic factor deficiency and from CUBN/AMBN mutations causing Imerslund-Gräsbeck syndrome, providing a new diagnostic entity in the spectrum of hereditary megaloblastic anaemias.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

**Maternal Iron Homeostasis: Comprehensive Review.** Zhang, Nemeth, and Sangkhae published in *BJH* a detailed review of maternal iron homeostasis, disorders, and pregnancy outcomes[^13]. The authors note that iron deficiency anaemia affects 12–80% of pregnant women depending on geographic region and represents the leading cause of years lived with disability in women worldwide. The review synthesises evidence on maternal, placental, and fetal iron-regulatory mechanisms, evaluates the limitations of available biomarkers (noting that serum ferritin may be suppressed in pregnancy independently of iron stores), and discusses the evidence base for intravenous iron therapy in the second and third trimesters. A key clinical point is the authors' call for early and repeated screening for iron deficiency throughout gestation rather than relying solely on haemoglobin-based anaemia diagnosis, as tissue iron deficiency precedes frank anaemia and may independently affect fetal neurodevelopment. Iron overload in pregnancy, although far less common, is also associated with adverse placental and fetal outcomes and is highlighted as underrecognised. For NCKUH obstetrician-haematology services, this review provides a current evidence synthesis supporting proactive iron screening and treatment protocols aligned with the authors' recommendations.

**Iron Deficiency in Daratumumab-Treated Multiple Myeloma.** Although primarily relevant to the malignant haematology domain, a *BJH* retrospective case-control study by Hwa et al. identifies a high prevalence of iron deficiency (approximately 50% meeting ferritin <30 mcg/L criteria at 1 year) among newly diagnosed multiple myeloma patients receiving daratumumab-based regimens (Dara-IRD), compared with none of the VRD-treated controls meeting this threshold[^14]. Given that anaemia management is a shared concern for haematologists managing complex myeloma patients, and that unrecognised iron deficiency may compromise the interpretation of haemoglobin trends on treatment, this finding has practical relevance across service lines. Routine ferritin monitoring at 6–12 months is a clinically actionable recommendation arising from this data.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

**Abelacimab FXI Inhibitor and Prior Anticoagulation Experience — AZALEA-TIMI 71 Subanalysis.** Small, Patel, Giugliano, Morrow et al. published in *Blood* a prespecified subgroup analysis of the AZALEA-TIMI 71 trial (NCT04755283) examining whether prior oral anticoagulation (OAC) experience modifies the bleeding reduction conferred by abelacimab, a fully human monoclonal antibody FXI inhibitor, in patients with atrial fibrillation[^2]. The central finding is that prior OAC experience is an independent predictor of bleeding risk in this population, and that abelacimab reduced bleeding regardless of prior OAC exposure. Notably, OAC-naïve patients appeared to derive potentially greater absolute bleeding reduction benefit. This finding is clinically significant for several reasons: first, it validates prior OAC experience as a clinically meaningful risk stratification tool within FXI inhibitor trials; second, it suggests that patients who have been OAC-naïve (due to physician or patient reluctance driven by bleeding concerns) may represent the population most likely to benefit from the superior bleeding profile of FXI inhibition compared to factor Xa or thrombin inhibition. Full quantitative data including hazard ratios and confidence intervals for the subgroup interaction will be important to evaluate when the complete manuscript is accessible. The AZALEA-TIMI 71 programme — which previously demonstrated approximately 80% relative bleeding reduction for abelacimab versus rivaroxaban — continues to generate practice-informing subsidiary analyses.

**Autoimmune Neutropenia in Adults — "How I Treat" Framework.** Leaf and Sykes published in *Blood* the first dedicated clinical management framework for adult autoimmune neutropenia (AiN)[^1]. This disorder, although less common than its paediatric counterpart, is clinically distinct: it is predominantly chronic rather than self-limited, occurs disproportionately in women and in those with co-existing autoimmune conditions, and anti-neutrophil antibodies are detectable in only approximately 50% of patients — a finding the authors contextualise as potentially reflecting both testing sensitivity limitations and the existence of cell-mediated (T cell–driven) mechanisms of neutrophil destruction in a significant subset. The authors emphasise that most patients with adult AiN, including those with absolute neutrophil counts (ANC) persistently below 500/µL, remain asymptomatic and infection-free, justifying observation as the primary management strategy in the absence of clinically significant infections. Where treatment is required, the framework progresses from G-CSF for acute infection management through immunosuppressive approaches (including cyclosporine and azathioprine) to T cell–directed strategies for refractory cases. Importantly, emerging therapies discussed include plasma cell–targeted approaches (relevant given the context of BCMA CAR-T and anti-CD38 strategies being applied in related autoimmune cytopenias), cytokine pathway modulators (including JAK inhibitors), and complement blockade — all supported by case-level evidence. For NCKUH haematologists, this review provides the most current algorithmic guidance available for a condition that, while rare, is encountered in outpatient haematology clinics and frequently poses diagnostic and therapeutic uncertainty.

**Sickle Cell Disease: RAAS Inhibitors Exacerbate Anaemia.** Eskandari et al. published in *Blood* a study demonstrating that renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitor (RAASi) use is independently associated with lower haemoglobin in sickle cell disease (SCD)[^15]. In a cross-sectional analysis of 658 Walk-PHaSST participants, RAASi use was associated with haemoglobin reduction of −0.46 ± 0.21 g/dL (p = 0.032) after adjustment for age, sex, SCD genotype, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and erythroid-stimulating agent and hydroxyurea use. Two longitudinal cohorts corroborated this signal: in a University of Illinois Chicago cohort (n = 24), RAASi therapy led to haemoglobin reduction of −0.44 ± 0.14 g/dL (p = 0.006), and in a multicenter losartan clinical trial cohort (n = 32), the reduction was −0.53 ± 0.17 g/dL (p = 0.005). Mechanistic studies in SCD mice treated with losartan showed lower haemoglobin concentrations at both 6 and 14 weeks (p <0.001), lower absolute reticulocyte counts by 14 weeks (p = 0.03), and reduced bone marrow erythroid:myeloid ratios, with rescue of erythroid colony formation upon exogenous erythropoietin supplementation (p = 0.02). Although RAASi remain the standard of care for SCD-related albuminuria, this study adds a quantified haemoglobin penalty to the risk-benefit calculation. Haematologists co-managing SCD patients who are initiated on ACE inhibitors or ARBs for nephroprotection should monitor haemoglobin serially and consider erythropoietin supplementation if clinically significant worsening anaemia is observed.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings this week are of heightened relevance to haematological practice in Taiwan. Regarding thalassaemia and iron management, Taiwan carries alpha-thalassaemia trait in approximately 4–5% and beta-thalassaemia trait in approximately 1–2% of the population; the maternal iron homeostasis review[^13] emphasising early and repeated iron screening throughout gestation is particularly pertinent in an obstetric population with background thalassaemia trait, where haemoglobin-based screening alone may underestimate iron deficiency burden. G6PD deficiency affects approximately 3% of Taiwanese males, and although no new G6PD-specific data were published this week, the sickle cell/haemolytic anaemia paradigm data on Band 3 autoantibody persistence post-malaria treatment[^11] is relevant for managing imported malaria cases seen at regional referral centres.

With regard to NHI coverage, eltrombopag and romiplostim are both available under Taiwan's National Health Insurance for refractory ITP; the 2-year aplastic anaemia romiplostim data[^8] will support coverage continuations for this indication. Eculizumab is covered for PNH under NHI special approval, and iptacopan is under regulatory review in Taiwan following EMA and FDA approvals. Abelacimab (FXI inhibitor) is not yet approved in Taiwan, and clinicians are advised to follow international regulatory agency decisions, which are anticipated in 2026–2027 based on AZALEA-TIMI 71 filing timelines.

The autoimmune neutropenia "How I Treat"[^1] is clinically timely for NCKUH, where AiN in the context of connective tissue disorders (lupus, Sjögren's syndrome — both prevalent in Taiwan's female population) is a recognised management challenge. The framework's evidence-based hierarchy of observation → G-CSF → immunosuppression → emerging cellular and cytokine-directed therapies provides clear guidance for local practice protocols.

The bevacizumab data for gastrointestinal vascular malformations[^10] is directly applicable to NCKUH's combined gastroenterology–haematology vascular malformation service, and the institutional pathway design reported by the authors could be adapted for a Taiwan-based protocol given the availability of bevacizumab on NHI for oncological indications.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

Adult autoimmune neutropenia is a predominantly chronic, female-predominant disorder in which nearly half of patients lack detectable anti-neutrophil antibodies; most patients can be safely observed without active treatment even at ANC below 500/µL, and T cell–directed and plasma cell–targeted emerging therapies now extend the therapeutic toolkit beyond G-CSF for refractory cases[^1]. The FXI inhibitor abelacimab reduces bleeding in atrial fibrillation regardless of prior OAC experience, with the potentially greatest absolute benefit in OAC-naïve patients — an important finding for guiding patient selection as this novel anticoagulant class approaches regulatory approval[^2]. Recombinant human thrombopoietin achieves sustained platelet thresholds before invasive procedures in 85.0% of patients with chronic liver disease–related thrombocytopenia, avoiding platelet transfusion in 92.5% — establishing a new phase III evidence base for this indication[^3]. L-carnitine–driven fatty acid oxidation via the PPARα–TFEB axis is a newly identified and potentially targetable metabolic circuit governing HSPC regeneration, with proof-of-concept rescue in aplastic anaemia patient–derived cells[^4]. Biallelic *BPNT1* loss-of-function defines a new genetic aetiology of vitamin B12–dependent megaloblastic anaemia mediated through PAP accumulation and impaired cubilin/amnionless ileal expression, warranting genetic workup in recurrent unexplained B12-responsive anaemia[^12]. RAASi use in sickle cell disease exacerbates anaemia by approximately 0.5 g/dL through an erythropoietin-reversible bone marrow suppression mechanism, requiring haemoglobin monitoring when nephrological indications mandate RAAS blockade[^15].

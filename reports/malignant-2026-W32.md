## I. AML — Acute Myeloid Leukemia

### FLT3-ITD Microclones: A Clinically Significant Prognostic Entity Challenging Current Risk Models

Current European LeukemiaNet (ELN) and international AML risk models categorize FLT3-ITD primarily according to allelic ratio (AR) thresholds, typically defining macroclones as AR ≥0.05. Low-level FLT3-ITD subclones below this threshold—"microclones"—have been largely dismissed as clinically inconsequential. A comprehensive post-hoc analysis of the prospective Backbone Intergroup 1 trial (NCT02416388), published in *Blood* this week, enrolled 1,733 patients with newly diagnosed AML and performed next-generation sequencing to detect FLT3-ITD at high sensitivity[^4]. FLT3-ITD microclones (AR between 0.0004 and 0.05) were identified in 17.4% of patients who lacked a FLT3-ITD macroclone. In multivariable analysis adjusted for age, white blood cell count, co-mutations, midostaurin treatment, and allogeneic HCT, microclones were independently associated with increased relapse risk, with a cause-specific hazard ratio of 1.50 (95% CI, 1.18–1.91). This was remarkably similar to the risk conferred by low-AR macroclones (cHR 1.98; 95% CI, 1.50–2.62) and somewhat below that of high-AR macroclones (cHR 2.33; 95% CI, 1.69–3.22). The 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse reached 45.1% (95% CI, 38.3–51.6) in patients with microclones, nearly identical to the 42.5% (95% CI, 37.0–47.9) observed with macroclones, and markedly higher than the 29.4% (95% CI, 26.6–32.3) in FLT3-ITD-negative patients. In NPM1-mutated AML, both microclones and macroclones were associated with higher MRD levels and elevated relapse risk, though without independent OS impact after MRD adjustment. Critically, 41.8% of relapses in patients with microclones at diagnosis were associated with a clonal expansion to macroclone status at relapse, suggesting selective evolutionary pressure during therapy. These findings carry direct clinical implications: routine NGS-based FLT3-ITD detection at high sensitivity, rather than fragment analysis alone, should be considered standard practice, and prospective trials addressing the role of FLT3 inhibitor escalation or post-remission intensification specifically in microclone-positive patients are urgently needed.

### Motixafortide Consolidation in AML: Negative Trial with a Positive Biomarker Signal

The CXCR4–CXCL12 axis is a well-characterized mechanism by which AML blasts evade cytarabine-based chemotherapy by anchoring in bone marrow niches. Motixafortide, a selective CXCR4 antagonist, was hypothesized to mobilize residual leukemia from the marrow microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of high-dose cytarabine (HiDAC) consolidation. Results from the randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial (NCT02502968) are now published in *Blood*[^5]. The trial enrolled 128 patients in first remission who received HiDAC plus motixafortide or placebo. The primary endpoint—median relapse-free survival—did not substantially differ between arms: 10.3 months (95% CI, 8.0–12.0) for motixafortide versus 11.5 months (95% CI, 8.6–24.1) for placebo (log-rank p = 0.98), a clearly negative result in the unselected population. However, a sophisticated single-cell MRD (scMRD) biomarker analysis conducted before consolidation revealed important treatment heterogeneity. In the placebo group, higher CXCR4 expression on residual leukemic cells was associated with increased relapse risk (p = 0.02), whereas in the motixafortide group, paradoxically, higher CXCR4 expression was linked to reduced relapse rate (p = 0.047), suggesting that CXCR4-high patients specifically benefit from CXCR4 inhibition. Exploratory analyses additionally identified scMRD thresholds at which higher pre-consolidation MRD burden correlated with inferior OS. Although these biomarker findings are hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation, they provide a rational framework for biomarker-driven patient selection in future consolidation trials combining CXCR4 antagonism with post-remission therapy.

### Ziftomenib Preclinical Characterization: Menin Inhibitor Activity Against Resistance Mutations

A detailed preclinical mechanistic paper published in *Blood* characterizes the activity of ziftomenib, the recently FDA-approved menin–KMT2A inhibitor for NPM1-mutated AML, across a panel of resistance-associated MEN1 mutations[^6]. Against the T349M mutant—the most clinically relevant resistance variant to the related inhibitor revumenib—ziftomenib retained full antileukemic activity. Against G331R cells, ziftomenib demonstrated low nanomolar potency (50% growth inhibition ≤25 nM), representing only several-fold reduction compared with MEN1 wild-type cells. In contrast, M327I and G331D mutants were resistant to ziftomenib. Crystal structure analyses of the ziftomenib–menin complex confirmed a distinct binding configuration at these mutant residues that rationalizes the differential activity profile. These data are clinically informative for sequencing menin inhibitors or designing combination strategies in the post-revumenib setting, and suggest that ziftomenib may offer salvage activity in a subset of patients progressing on other menin inhibitors.

### pDC-AML: ER Stress-Driven Immune Evasion and the Case for Early Allo-HSCT

A multi-omic characterization of plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation associated with AML (pDC-AML) published in *Leukemia* provides new mechanistic insight into the immune evasion mechanisms underlying this poor-prognosis entity[^7]. Investigators performed single-cell RNA-seq and proteomic analyses of 18 pDC-AML cases alongside 207 non-pDC-AML and 16 BPDCN controls. A key finding was that pDC-AML leukemia stem cells exhibited unfolded protein response (UPR) activation—specifically the IRE1α–XBP1 axis—which temporally preceded acquisition of the pDC maturation program. Pharmacological induction of ER stress in myeloid cells upregulated BCL11A, the master transcription factor of pDC differentiation, and induced a CD123+BDCA2+ pDC immunophenotype. Functionally, pDC-AML-derived pDCs showed compromised antigen presentation and markedly restricted T-cell clonal expansion in the bone marrow microenvironment, as demonstrated by scTCR-seq analysis, correlating with leukemic burden and reversing upon clinical remission. Clinically, allogeneic HSCT significantly improved both overall survival and progression-free survival in pDC-AML, abrogating the prognostic disadvantage relative to non-pDC-AML. These data support early consideration of allo-HSCT in eligible pDC-AML patients and identify the IRE1α–XBP1–BCL11A axis as a candidate therapeutic target.

## II. ALL — Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

### Consensus Recommendations for CAR T-Cell Administration in Adult B-ALL

The increasing availability of three commercial CD19-directed CAR T-cell products for adult B-cell ALL has exposed significant gaps in evidence-based practice guidelines. A modified Delphi consensus study published in *Blood* engaged a panel of nine principal investigators from the Real World Outcomes Collaborative of CAR T-Cell Therapy in Adult ALL (ROCCA) consortium, with validation by 27 additional consortium PIs, to address key clinical questions including patient selection, bridging therapy, lymphodepletion strategies, treatment setting, toxicity management, post-infusion MRD monitoring, and the role of consolidation or maintenance after CAR T-cell therapy[^8]. Of 58 initial recommendation statements evaluated, 34 achieved expert panel consensus after two formal panel meetings, and 33 of these were subsequently validated by the broader ROCCA group. While the specific statements are detailed in the publication, the consensus framework represents the most comprehensive practice guidance yet available for this rapidly evolving field and will be particularly valuable at centers building adult B-ALL CAR T programs. NCKUH clinicians engaged in the growing national CAR T program for ALL should review these recommendations as they benchmark emerging institutional protocols.

### LCK-Targeting Molecular Glue Degraders in T-ALL: Overcoming Kinase Inhibitor Resistance

A translational study published in *Blood* reports the development of cereblon-recruiting molecular glue degraders (MGDs) targeting lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase (LCK), an oncogenic dependency in T-cell ALL[^9]. Through high-throughput screening and medicinal chemistry optimization, investigators developed MGDs inducing CRBN-dependent LCK degradation with potent cytotoxicity in T-ALL cell lines and primary samples. The mechanistic distinction from classical inhibitor-based approaches is important: unlike ATP-competitive inhibitors or PROTAC-type molecules, these MGDs engage LCK at regions distal to the ATP-binding pocket, and critically, they retain activity against gatekeeper LCK mutations (including those responsible for resistance to kinase inhibitor-based therapeutics). Structure-activity relationship modeling and ternary complex crystallography confirmed a non-canonical degron at the LCK–CRBN interface involving the G-loop. Although these findings are preclinical, they provide a conceptual framework for overcoming kinase inhibitor resistance in T-ALL through targeted protein degradation, an approach with broader applicability across drug-refractory hematologic malignancies.

## III. CML — Chronic Myeloid Leukemia

### BCR::ABL1 International Scale Standardization: A 15-Year Impact Assessment

_No new trial efficacy data this week._ However, a methodologically significant 15-year retrospective analysis of College of American Pathologists (CAP) BCR::ABL1 proficiency testing data, published in *Leukemia*, provides the first comprehensive evidence for the real-world clinical impact of International Scale (IS) adoption across more than 200 clinical laboratories[^10]. Between 2009 and 2023, IS adoption rose from 8% of participating laboratories to >99% by 2019, correlating with a progressive and statistically significant reduction in inter-laboratory standard deviations for high-sample (r = −0.73; p = 0.004), intermediate-sample (r = −0.71; p = 0.022), and low-sample (r = −0.84; p = 0.002) BCR::ABL1 results. No other variable—including reference gene selection, reagent type, assay platform, or practice setting—correlated with this time-dependent improvement. These data validate the IS as the principal driver of enhanced inter-laboratory reproducibility and carry direct implications for molecular monitoring practices: IS reporting must be considered obligatory in any clinical or trial context, and laboratories reporting non-IS BCR::ABL1 values should be identified and remediated. This is particularly relevant in Taiwan, where IS conversion factors are not uniformly applied across all academic and community molecular laboratories.

## IV. CLL / SLL — Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia

### ZMYM3 Mutations: A New Independent Adverse Prognostic Factor with Therapeutic Implications

A comprehensive translational study published in *Blood Advances* characterizes ZMYM3 mutations in a cohort of 487 CLL patients using next-generation sequencing, identifying 32 variants predominantly manifesting as loss-of-function events[^11]. ZMYM3-mutated CLL showed strong enrichment for NOTCH signaling pathway alterations, with 60% of ZMYM3-mutated cases co-harboring NOTCH1 mutations. Clinically, ZMYM3 mutations were associated with significantly shorter time to first treatment (median 35 vs. 53 months; p = 0.010) and inferior outcome among early-stage patients (median time to first treatment 48 vs. 108 months; p = 0.002). Multivariable analysis confirmed ZMYM3 mutations as an independent adverse prognostic factor. CRISPR/Cas9-edited CLL cell models demonstrated that ZMYM3 mutations cooperate with NOTCH1 mutations to induce widespread transcriptional dysregulation, reducing chromatin accessibility and promoting apoptosis evasion through caspase downregulation. Importantly, primary ZMYM3-mutated CLL cells exhibited increased sensitivity to BCL-XL inhibition and ibrutinib in vitro, raising the hypothesis that this molecular subgroup may benefit from specific therapeutic strategies. Inclusion of ZMYM3 status in routine CLL molecular profiling panels warrants prospective evaluation.

### PLCG2 Exon-Skipped Variants: Novel BTK Inhibitor Resistance Mechanism in CLL

A mechanistic study published in *Blood Advances* identifies a previously unreported class of PLCG2 splice site alterations generating exon-skipped variants that contribute to BTKi resistance in CLL beyond classical BTK or canonical PLCG2 point mutations[^12]. Investigators studying REC-1 cells with acquired resistance to tirabrutinib identified a splice site mutation (c.2236-1G>T) generating the Δ21 variant with enhanced phospholipase activity, and a corresponding deletion at the same locus was identified in a CLL patient progressing on ibrutinib. Analysis of primary CLL samples revealed widespread expression of additional variants (Δ22 and Δ20-22), with the active Δ20-22 variant significantly more prevalent in CLL compared with healthy donor samples. Treatment analysis showed that venetoclax exposure, but not ibrutinib, led to significant downregulation of Δ20-22 expression (p = 0.02), suggesting these splice variants may persist under BTKi selective pressure and be subject to negative selection by venetoclax. These findings expand the landscape of BTKi resistance mechanisms and suggest that resistance testing panels should incorporate splice-site variant detection, particularly in patients with CLL progression on BTK inhibitors who lack canonical resistance mutations.

## V. MDS — Myelodysplastic Syndromes

### Luspatercept in Higher-Risk MDS: Hematologic Response Data

A study published in *Blood Advances* reports hematologic responses achieved with luspatercept in higher-risk MDS, a population in which the agent has been less studied than in lower-risk disease[^13]. The manuscript provides clinical response data in this more aggressive disease context, though specific numerical outcomes in the published abstract are limited. Given the recent approval trajectory of luspatercept across MDS subtypes, these data will inform emerging higher-risk MDS treatment algorithms. Full data review indicates this represents real-world or expanded cohort experience rather than a new randomized trial.

### STAG2-Dependent Chromatin Remodeling and MDS Dyserythropoiesis: Mechanistic Insights

A translational study published in *Blood* establishes a mechanistic link between loss of STAG2—a cohesin complex member recurrently mutated in MDS—and aberrant GATA1 retargeting that underlies dyserythropoiesis[^14]. Comprehensive genomic studies in Stag2Δ mice demonstrated reduced erythroid progenitor numbers, impaired terminal erythroid differentiation, and increased megakaryocyte output. RNA-seq and ATAC-seq of erythroid progenitors revealed altered chromatin accessibility, with Stag2-deficient GATA1 occupancy reprogrammed from erythroid to megakaryocyte targets, with FLI1 motifs enriched at Stag2Δ GATA1 binding sites despite unchanged GATA1 expression levels. Functional rescue of the erythroid output deficit was partially achieved through FLI1 knockdown. Human primary MDS samples with STAG2 mutations recapitulated these phenotypic and molecular features. These findings establish chromatin accessibility—rather than transcription factor expression—as the key determinant of GATA1 cistrome specificity, and identify a cohesin-FLI1-GATA1 axis as a candidate therapeutic target in STAG2-mutated MDS.

## VI. MPN — Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (PV / ET / MF)

### PV-RR6 Prognostic Model: Early Identification of Ruxolitinib Treatment Failure in Polycythemia Vera

A prospective observational cohort study published in *Blood Advances* develops and validates the PV-RR6 (Polycythemia Vera–Response to Ruxolitinib at 6 months) prognostic model in 178 ruxolitinib-treated PV patients from the PV-ARC study[^15]. After a median follow-up of 3.50 years from ruxolitinib start, 5-year event-free survival (EFS; composite of post-PV MF progression, thrombosis, hemorrhage, or death) was 70.4%. Multivariable analysis identified three independent risk factors for inferior EFS: ruxolitinib dose <10 mg twice daily at one or more time points (HR 1.94; p = 0.047), absence of ≥50% spleen length reduction at months 3 and 6 (HR 2.65; p = 0.009), and phlebotomy requirement at ≥2 assessment time points (HR 2.11; p = 0.039). Incorporating these variables into a weighted scoring system stratified patients into three risk categories with markedly different 5-year EFS: low risk (score 0; EFS 89.4%; n = 63), intermediate risk (score 1–2.5; EFS 71.0%; n = 82), and high risk (score >2.5; EFS 38.9%; n = 33). The PV-RR6 model provides a simple, clinically actionable tool to identify PV patients at high risk of adverse outcomes on ruxolitinib as early as 6 months after treatment initiation, supporting timely consideration of treatment modification or escalation to clinical trials.

### Real-World Myelofibrosis Survival in the JAK Inhibitor Era: Persistent Mortality Gap

A large retrospective registry analysis of 1,649 MF patients diagnosed from 2010 onwards across 64 Spanish centers, published in the *British Journal of Haematology*, provides important real-world benchmarking data for the contemporary JAK inhibitor era[^16]. JAK inhibitors were administered to approximately half of all patients. Despite this, median survival was only 6.6 years with no significant difference between primary and secondary MF. Acute leukemia transformation was the most frequent cause of death, accounting for 20% of cases. Relative survival at 5 and 10 years was 68% and 44%, respectively, compared with a matched general population—a gap that was most pronounced in patients aged >65 years and male patients, two subgroups also carrying higher burdens of comorbidities and high molecular risk mutations. Strikingly, among patients aged ≤70 years with intermediate-2 or high-risk IPSS disease, only 25% ultimately underwent transplantation, highlighting the significant unmet need for improving access to potentially curative therapy. The 10-year relative survival of only 12% in high-risk IPSS patients—even in the ruxolitinib era—underscores the urgent need for novel agents and earlier HSCT integration.

### Non-invasive Biomarker Discrimination of ET, Pre-MF, and Overt MF

A prospective observational study published in *Leukemia* evaluated a panel of non-invasive inflammatory mediators, immune activation markers, and matrix regulators to distinguish between 44 ET, 53 pre-MF, and 21 overt PMF patients[^17]. The mutational landscape showed increasing complexity across the diagnostic spectrum from ET to PMF. Significant differential expression was observed for inflammatory mediators (IL-1RA, IL-1β, IL-6, S100A8/S100A9, CCL4), immune activation markers (CD25, CXCL10), matrix regulators (YLK-40, TIMP1), and growth factors (EGF, CXCL4). A two-step Lasso regression strategy with internal bootstrap validation achieved an AUC of 0.909 (95% CI, 0.804–0.994) for identifying overt PMF and an AUC of 0.762 (95% CI, 0.656–0.847) for discriminating pre-MF from ET. While the discriminatory power for the ET/pre-MF distinction falls short of clinical implementation, the high accuracy for PMF identification supports the development of non-invasive diagnostic panels that may reduce dependence on bone marrow biopsy in patients for whom the procedure carries higher risk.

## VII. DLBCL & Aggressive B-cell Lymphoma

### STARGLO Three-Year Follow-up: Durable Superiority of Glofitamab-GemOx in ASCT-Ineligible R/R DLBCL

The phase 3 STARGLO trial demonstrated that glofitamab-GemOx (Glofit-GemOx) improved OS compared with rituximab-GemOx (R-GemOx) in ASCT-ineligible relapsed/refractory DLBCL at primary analysis. Updated results with a median OS follow-up of 35.1 months (data cutoff: 1 May 2025), now published in *Blood Advances*, confirm the durability of this benefit[^3]. The median OS was 25.5 months for Glofit-GemOx versus 12.5 months for R-GemOx (HR 0.60; 95% CI, 0.4–0.8), and median PFS was 14.4 versus 3.3 months (HR 0.41; 95% CI, 0.3–0.6). The complete response rate was 58.5% versus 25.3%. Outcomes were particularly favorable in the second-line setting, where the 3-year OS rate reached 54.6% (95% CI, 45.2–64.0%) for Glofit-GemOx compared with 33.2% (95% CI, 21.2–45.3%) for third-line or later patients. Among second-line patients with primary refractory disease or early treatment failure—a historically poor-prognosis group—the 36-month OS rate was 46.1% (95% CI, 35.2–56.9%) with Glofit-GemOx versus 16.5% (95% CI, 3.4–29.6%) with R-GemOx. Consistent efficacy was observed across all age categories, including patients aged ≥75 years, and no new safety signals were identified. These data firmly establish fixed-duration Glofit-GemOx as a standard-of-care option with curative potential in the second-line ASCT-ineligible setting, and support its consideration for patients unsuitable for or failing CAR T-cell therapy.

### Real-World CAR T Uptake in LBCL: Significant Access Gap Despite Curative Potential

A large retrospective analysis utilizing the Flatiron Health Research Database, published in *Blood Advances*, evaluated treatment patterns and survival among 10,016 LBCL patients initiating first-line therapy from 2011 to 2024[^18]. Among all first-line patients, 13% died within one year without receiving second-line therapy, and 30% ultimately initiated second-line treatment. Critically, among patients deemed fit for CAR T-cell therapy (ECOG PS 0/1), only 25% received second-line CAR T and only 36% received third-line CAR T despite FDA approval and curative intent. Competing risk analyses identified 11% of ECOG PS 0/1 patients dying within 12 months of first-line initiation before having the opportunity for second-line therapy. These real-world data highlight a profound access gap that is not explained by biological unsuitability alone, pointing to delays in referral, geographic disparities, financial barriers, and the rapidity of disease progression. The findings reinforce the urgency of early collaboration between community oncologists and CAR T centers.

### Fludarabine Exposure Optimization for CAR T-Cell Outcomes in LBCL

A prospective single-center pharmacokinetic study published in *Blood Advances* prospectively measured fludarabine area under the curve (AUC) in 54 patients with relapsed/refractory LBCL receiving CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy[^19]. The median fludarabine AUC was 18.12 mg·h/L (IQR, 14.56–20.55). Product-specific optimal AUC exposure windows were identified: 17.5–21.5 mg·h/L for axicabtagene ciloleucel and 14.0–18.0 mg·h/L for tisagenlecleucel, with 44% of patients (n = 24) falling within the optimal range. Median PFS was not reached in the optimal exposure cohort versus 13.3 months in the suboptimal cohort (p = 0.03), and in multivariable analysis, optimal fludarabine exposure remained independently associated with improved PFS (p = 0.03), alongside axi-cel product selection (p = 0.03). CAR T-cell expansion, CRS rates, ICANS incidence, and hematotoxicity were comparable between groups, suggesting that optimal dosing improves efficacy without increasing toxicity. These data provide a compelling rationale for prospective PK-guided fludarabine dosing in LBCL patients receiving CAR T, and institutions with pharmacokinetic monitoring capabilities should consider implementing this approach.

## VIII. Indolent Lymphoma (FL, MCL, MZL)

### Mosunetuzumab Plus Polatuzumab Vedotin in Relapsed/Refractory MCL: First Bispecific-ADC Combination Data

The combination of mosunetuzumab (anti-CD20 bispecific antibody) and polatuzumab vedotin (anti-CD79b ADC) targets the malignant B cell through two mechanistically independent cytotoxic pathways, an approach now evaluated in a high-risk MCL population in a multicenter phase 2 trial published in *Blood*[^2]. The 42 enrolled patients had received a median of 3 prior lines of therapy, all including a BTK inhibitor; 26% had prior CAR T-cell therapy. The population was notably high-risk: 67% had Ki-67 ≥50%, 38% had blastoid/pleomorphic morphology, and 48% harbored TP53 aberrations. The centrally assessed best objective response rate was 88.1% (95% CI, 74.4–96.0%), with a complete response rate of 78.6% (95% CI, 63.2–89.7%). With a median follow-up of 15.9 months, the median PFS was 18.6 months (95% CI, 13.9 to not estimable). Efficacy was consistent across high-risk subgroups, including TP53-aberrant disease. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in 42.9% of patients but was limited to grade 1/2 events. These data, representing the first bispecific-ADC combination study in MCL, demonstrate a compelling efficacy signal with a manageable outpatient-compatible safety profile in a post-BTKi population with very limited treatment options. The CR rate of 78.6% in TP53-aberrant MCL is particularly noteworthy given the historically dismal outcomes in this subgroup.

### Stage IA NLPHL: Radiotherapy Alone Sufficient; Rituximab Monotherapy Insufficient for Durable Control

The Global NLPHL One Working Group has published a large retrospective analysis of 779 patients with stage IA nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL) in *Blood Advances*[^20]. Among patients with completely resected disease, observation alone yielded a 6-year PFS of 65.5% compared with 90.5% for those receiving consolidative radiotherapy (RT), supporting adjuvant RT even after macroscopically complete excision. For the larger unresected disease group (n = 627), the 6-year PFS was 62.0% for rituximab monotherapy (n = 31), 89.6% for RT alone (n = 325), 76.8% for ABVD alone (n = 40), and 94.3% for ABVD plus RT (n = 130). Notably, chemotherapy added to RT did not provide statistically meaningful PFS benefit over RT alone, while rituximab or ABVD monotherapy fell substantially short of RT in durability. With 127 total relapses (16.3%), of which 19.7% transformed histologically, these data support RT alone as the preferred definitive treatment for stage IA NLPHL, with limited role for rituximab monotherapy in this setting.

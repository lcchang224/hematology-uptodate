## Summary

This cycle brings a landmark guideline update and several mechanistically important papers reshaping the immune thrombocytopenia, antiphospholipid syndrome, and PNH landscapes. First and most significant, ASH has published its 2026 focused update to the ITP treatment guidelines, now recommending initial therapy with rituximab plus corticosteroids or a thrombopoietin receptor agonist (TPO-RA) plus corticosteroids rather than corticosteroid monotherapy (conditional recommendation), and a thrombopoietic agent (strong recommendation) or rituximab (conditional recommendation) for patients failing first-line steroids — a substantive departure from the 2019 guidance that will directly affect first-line prescribing practice[^1]. Second, a *Blood* paper fundamentally reframes antiphospholipid syndrome pathogenesis, demonstrating that platelet factor 4 (PF4) bridges β2-glycoprotein I to neutrophil extracellular traps to form a prothrombotic PF4:β2GPI:NET immunocomplex that is the actual target of pathogenic APS antibodies, with thrombus size reduced by ADAMTS13 or DNase I infusion in microfluidic whole-blood models and thrombosis abolished in PF4-knockout mice, opening a nonanticoagulant therapeutic avenue[^2],[^3]. Third, a companion *Blood Advances* mechanistic study shows that anti-PF4 antibodies (as in vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, VITT) trigger a faster and stronger platelet procoagulant response than classical anti-heparin/PF4 antibodies of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, providing a biological explanation for the greater thrombotic severity of VITT compared with HIT[^4]. Fourth, a 227-patient multicenter *BJH* study of classical PNH managed without complement inhibitor access shows that allogeneic HSCT achieves 96.08% normalized complete blood counts at 6 months versus 3.41% with non-transplant therapy (p<0.001) and 92.16% 5-year failure-free survival versus 0.57% (p<0.001), with no significant 5-year overall survival difference (92.16% vs 98.83%, p=0.097) — important historical-control data for regions where complement inhibitors remain inaccessible[^5]. Fifth, a *Haematologica* genetic-screening editorial argues for moving ITP diagnostics "beyond exclusion" toward proactive inherited thrombocytopenia gene panels, complementing a parallel *BJH* biomarker study identifying CD19+CD38+ regulatory B cells as a predictor of steroid-induced remission in treatment-naïve adult ITP[^6],[^7].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

This week delivers the most consequential ITP update of the year: the full ASH 2026 focused guideline update, evaluating evidence through 19 July 2025 via GRADE methodology, now recommends combination initial therapy — rituximab plus corticosteroids or a TPO-RA plus corticosteroids — over corticosteroid monotherapy in adults with primary ITP, a conditional recommendation reflecting improved durable response rates without excess toxicity signal in the pooled evidence base[^1]. For second-line therapy after corticosteroid failure, the panel issued a strong recommendation for a thrombopoietic agent and a conditional recommendation for rituximab, alongside good-practice statements addressing TPO-RA switching strategies and splenectomy timing, representing the first substantive revision of first-line ITP management since the 2019 guidelines[^1]. Complementing this update, a prospective single-center cohort of 37 treatment-naïve adult ITP patients receiving standardized dexamethasone found that a baseline CD19+CD38+ B-cell percentage above 2.6% independently predicted steroid-induced remission at 12 weeks (p<0.001 on multivariate analysis), with complete response achieved in 43% of the cohort overall, suggesting a candidate biomarker to stratify steroid responsiveness before committing to prolonged corticosteroid courses[^7]. A *Haematologica* editorial separately makes the case for incorporating genetic screening into ITP diagnostic algorithms rather than reserving molecular testing purely to exclude inherited thrombocytopenias when clinical suspicion is high, arguing that misclassification of inherited thrombocytopenia as refractory ITP exposes patients to unnecessary and ineffective immunosuppression[^6]. A related *Haematologica* pharmacovigilance study examines the risk of immune thrombocytopenia following COVID-19 vaccination versus SARS-CoV-2 infection itself, contributing further population-level reassurance data to this now well-studied association, although the available abstract did not provide extractable effect estimates in this cache[^8].

## II. TTP / HUS / Thrombotic Microangiopathy

A *Blood Advances* commentary this week, "Mortality prediction in immune TTP remains challenging despite artificial intelligence advances," critically appraises recent machine-learning mortality-prediction models in iTTP, underscoring that despite methodological sophistication, clinical adoption remains limited by small training cohorts and lack of external validation across ethnically diverse populations[^9]. This cautionary framing is a useful counterpoint to the broader momentum toward AI-based risk stratification in TMA and reinforces last cycle's argument that single-parameter and even algorithmic remission/mortality thresholds in TTP require careful validation before guiding urgent plasma exchange or caplacizumab decisions[^10]. No new primary outcome data (ADAMTS13 trial results, caplacizumab real-world cohorts) were identified this cycle beyond this methodologic commentary.

## IV. PNH — Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria

A multicenter *BJH* retrospective study spanning ten centers and 227 patients with classical PNH treated between January 1976 and April 2022 — specifically in a setting without access to complement inhibitors — provides important historical benchmarking data for HSCT versus supportive non-transplant care[^5]. Among 51 patients undergoing HSCT versus 176 receiving non-transplant treatment, normalization of complete blood counts at 6 months occurred in 96.08% of HSCT recipients compared with only 3.41% of non-transplant patients (p<0.001), and 5-year failure-free survival was 92.16% in the HSCT cohort versus just 0.57% in the non-transplant cohort (p<0.001)[^5]. Notably, 5-year overall survival did not differ significantly between the two strategies (92.16% vs 98.83%, p=0.097), and alternative-donor HSCT achieved outcomes comparable to matched sibling donor transplantation, while timing of transplant within versus beyond 12 months of diagnosis did not significantly affect delayed platelet engraftment-free, GVHD-free, failure-free survival (83.33% vs 62.34%, p=0.136)[^5]. The authors appropriately caveat that these data derive from a complement-inhibitor-naïve era and that the role of HSCT in PNH management requires reassessment now that eculizumab, ravulizumab, and iptacopan are widely available, but the findings remain directly relevant to health systems or individual patients where complement inhibitor access is constrained — a scenario of practical relevance for resource-limited settings. No new iptacopan, eculizumab, or ravulizumab trial data appeared in the indexed literature this cycle beyond archival EHA News conference abstracts already covered in prior reports[^11],[^12].

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

Two important hemophilia A papers appear this week. First, a *Blood* case report from the ASC618 phase 1/2 trial (NCT04676048) describes high-titer FVIII inhibitor formation in both treated patients following gene therapy with ET3, a bioengineered chimeric human/porcine B-domain-deleted FVIII transgene; despite initial factor expression, both patients developed high-titer inhibitors that remain elevated nearly two years after single infusion and are now managed with emicizumab prophylaxis, with the trial halted as a result[^13]. The authors attribute this to immunogenic epitopes within the human-porcine hybrid construct to which neither patient — despite more than 1000 prior FVIII exposure days — had developed immune tolerance, a sobering lesson for bioengineered transgene design in next-generation AAV gene therapy programs[^13]. Second, a *Blood Advances* editorial, "A next-generation FVIIIa mimetic in the making," discusses the trajectory of FVIIIa-mimetic bispecific antibody development following emicizumab, contextualizing ongoing preclinical work (including the HuHA mouse model reported in last cycle's report) within the broader push toward improved pharmacokinetics and reduced immunogenicity compared with first-generation bispecifics[^14],[^15]. Separately, a *BJH* genotype-phenotype study examines platelet receptor genotypes as modifiers of bleeding severity phenotype in hemophilia A, and a *Haematologica* preclinical paper assesses the effects of secondary FVIII prophylaxis on joint and bone health outcomes in a hemophilia A mouse model, though neither abstract in this cache provided extractable quantitative results[^16],[^17]. A *Blood Advances* editorial on venous thromboembolism occurring during hemophilia treatment, "Thromboprophylaxis and the treatment paradox in HA-VTE," highlights the emerging clinical tension of managing thrombotic risk in hemophilia patients receiving increasingly hemostatically effective non-factor therapies such as emicizumab and concizumab, though the underlying primary data were not available in the abstract cache[^18].

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

A *Blood Advances* review this week addresses the emerging role of genetic disease modifiers (GDMs) in β-hemoglobinopathies, arguing that GDM characterization is becoming essential not only as a target for curative gene-editing therapies but also for customizing therapy choice, planning curative approaches, and ensuring equitable global access to these high-cost interventions across ethnic groups with differing modifier allele frequencies[^19]. This is directly relevant to Taiwan's thalassemia population, where modifier loci such as BCL11A and HBS1L-MYB variants influence fetal hemoglobin induction response and would be expected to affect eligibility and expected benefit from gene therapies such as exagamglobene autotemcel as these become more widely available regionally. Separately, a *BJH* study demonstrates that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation produces sustained reversal of sickle cell cardiomyopathy superior to disease-modifying therapy, with cardiac MRI in 20 HSCT recipients showing progressive normalization of extracellular volume (a myocardial fibrosis surrogate) by 36 months post-transplant and improved diastolic parameters compared with age-matched patients on disease-modifying therapy at 12 months[^20]; while this cohort is sickle cell disease rather than thalassemia, the cardiac remodeling principles are mechanistically relevant to transfusion-dependent thalassemia populations followed at NCKUH's thalassemia clinic given shared iron-overload cardiomyopathy pathophysiology.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

A *Blood* mechanistic study provides new insight into the vascular consequences of iron deficiency anemia (IDA), demonstrating in a murine model that IDA increases arterial nitric oxide signaling in female mice via loss of functional heme-bound (holo-) endothelial α-globin, an effect fully rescued by vascular iron repletion with iron dextran, whereas male mice showed no comparable NO signaling change despite similar anemia severity and α-globin protein reduction[^21]. Using endothelial-specific α-globin knockout mice, the authors confirm that loss of endothelial α-globin is mechanistically necessary for both the IDA-induced NO signaling increase and its reversal by iron repletion, establishing a sex-dimorphic vascular consequence of iron deficiency with implications for understanding cardiovascular risk patterns in IDA beyond anemia severity alone[^21]. This adds mechanistic depth to the clinical observation that iron deficiency, independent of hemoglobin level, is an independent driver of cardiovascular morbidity, relevant to the common NCKUH outpatient scenario of iron-deficient but non-anemic patients with unexplained vascular symptoms.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

This cycle's most important mechanistic advance is a *Blood* study fundamentally reframing antiphospholipid syndrome as a PF4-centric immunothrombotic disorder rather than a disease defined purely by anti-β2GPI antibody binding[^2]. The authors demonstrate using dynamic light-scattering of IgG from four triple-positive APS patients that PF4 bridges β2GPI to neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), forming a multimolecular PF4:β2GPI:NET complex that constitutes the actual antigenic target recognized by pathogenic APS antibodies; in a microfluidic whole-blood thrombosis model using photochemically injured, endothelium-lined channels, platelet-, fibrin-, and complement-rich thrombi bound APS antibodies only in the presence of PF4, and thrombus size was reduced by infusion of either ADAMTS13 or DNase I[^2]. In a murine APS model, both wild-type mice and transgenic mice expressing human PF4 (with or without FcγRIIA) developed more intense neutrophil rolling and more extensive laser-injury thrombosis, an effect abolished in PF4-knockout mice, and three antigenically distinct anti-human-PF4 monoclonal antibodies blocked both neutrophil rolling and thrombosis in vivo — pointing toward nonanticoagulant, PF4-targeted therapeutic strategies as a plausible future direction in APS management[^2]. An accompanying *Blood* editorial, "Reframing APS thrombogenesis: a new PF4-driven mechanism," contextualizes these findings within the broader family of PF4-driven immunothrombotic disorders that already includes heparin-induced thrombocytopenia and vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis[^3]. Directly complementing this mechanistic advance, a *Blood Advances* study compares the intrinsic prothrombotic properties of monoclonal IgG1 antibodies modeling anti-PF4 (VITT-type, clone 1E12) versus anti-PF4/heparin (HIT-type, clone 5B9) antibodies, finding that anti-PF4 antibodies induced significantly faster and stronger platelet activation by serotonin release assay, greater potentiation of thrombin generation (dependent on platelets plus coagulation factors V and XI), and more pronounced formation of procoagulant CD62P+/phosphatidylserine+ platelets and procoagulant microvesicles compared with anti-PF4/heparin antibodies, providing a biologically plausible explanation for why VITT and VITT-like syndromes manifest more severe and more frequent thrombosis than classical HIT[^4]. A further *Blood Advances* paper using a sepsis-DVT mouse model shows that mast cells are dispensable for venous thrombus initiation under septic (lipopolysaccharide-induced) conditions but are required for NET-rich thrombus stability, resistance to fibrinolysis, and pulmonary thromboinflammation, with mast-cell-deficient (KitW-sh) mice showing thrombi more susceptible to lysis by combined tissue plasminogen activator and DNase I and lower intrathrombus citrullinated histone H3 and IL-1β levels than wild-type controls[^22]. Finally, a zebrafish-to-human translational study identifies miR-150 (via downregulation of MASTL, a regulator of P2Y12 downstream signaling) as a modulator of clopidogrel responsiveness, with the human orthologous miRNAs miR-17-5p and miR-106a-5p found significantly upregulated in clopidogrel-resistant patients, offering a mechanistic candidate biomarker for antiplatelet therapy non-response relevant to cardiovascular patients co-managed with hematology for bleeding-thrombosis risk balancing[^23].

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

The ASH 2026 ITP guideline update carries direct implications for NCKUH practice: the shift toward combination first-line therapy (rituximab or TPO-RA plus corticosteroids) rather than corticosteroid monotherapy will require institutional discussion given that eltrombopag and avatrombopag are NHI-reimbursed in Taiwan only after documented corticosteroid or IVIG failure under current local reimbursement criteria, creating a potential mismatch between the new international first-line recommendation and existing NHI second-line gating criteria that merits proactive engagement with the NHI Pharmaceutical Benefit and Reimbursement Scheme committee[^1]. The PNH HSCT-versus-complement-inhibitor-free-management data are of limited direct applicability to Taiwan given that eculizumab and ravulizumab are both NHI-reimbursed for classical PNH with hemolysis, but the comparative failure-free survival figures remain a useful historical reference point for counseling the rare patient who declines or cannot tolerate complement inhibitor therapy[^5]. The ASC618 inhibitor-formation cases reinforce the importance of careful post-infusion immunogenicity monitoring protocols as Taiwan's hemophilia centers, including NCKUH, begin or continue participation in AAV-based gene therapy trials and real-world valoctocogene roxaparvovec administration; FVIII inhibitor screening schedules should anticipate the possibility of delayed, high-titer inhibitor emergence even in heavily pretreated patients with extensive prior factor exposure[^13]. With alpha-thalassemia carrier prevalence of approximately 4–5% and beta-thalassemia carrier prevalence of 1–2% in the Taiwanese population, the genetic-disease-modifier review is directly relevant to pretransplant and pre-gene-therapy counseling for thalassemia major patients followed at NCKUH, particularly regarding anticipated variability in fetal hemoglobin response to gene-editing therapies based on modifier haplotype[^19].

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The ASH 2026 ITP guideline update is this cycle's single most actionable finding and should prompt an institutional multidisciplinary review of first-line ITP management pathways and corresponding NHI reimbursement alignment[^1]. The PF4-centric reconceptualization of antiphospholipid syndrome, reinforced by the anti-PF4 versus anti-PF4/heparin procoagulant comparison, represents a genuine paradigm shift with potential future nonanticoagulant therapeutic implications, though clinical translation remains distant[^2],[^4]. The ASC618 inhibitor cases are a cautionary signal for the broader hemophilia A gene therapy field regarding bioengineered transgene immunogenicity, while the PNH HSCT outcome data, though derived from a pre-complement-inhibitor era, offer useful comparative benchmarks for resource-constrained settings[^13],[^5].

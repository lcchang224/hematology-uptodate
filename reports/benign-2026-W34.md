## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

**McMaster Guideline — Formal Publication Confirmed.** The guideline on emergency management of critical bleeding in ITP discussed in last week's report has now appeared with its formal *Blood Advances* citation, confirming the panel composition (5 adult ITP experts, 3 pediatric ITP experts, 2 emergency physicians, 1 emergency nurse, 2 methodologists, 4 patient partners) and the strong GRADE recommendations for combined corticosteroids, high-dose IVIG, platelet transfusion, tranexamic acid, and TPO-RAs, together with the conditional recommendation for urgent splenectomy after treatment failure and conditional recommendation against recombinant factor VIIa given thrombotic risk[^1]. No additional new clinical data beyond what was previously summarized have emerged this week; readers are referred to last week's report for the full narrative discussion. No new signal on the platelet-reactive antibody/PIFT study beyond what was already reported.

**MGTS Framework — Relevance to ITP-like Thrombocytopenia.** The *AJH* review proposing monoclonal gammopathy of thrombotic significance (MGTS) explicitly identifies monoclonal protein–induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis as the only disorder currently meeting the authors' criteria for direct, mechanistically supported M-protein causation of a thrombotic/thrombocytopenic phenotype[^5]. This has direct implications for the differential diagnosis of thrombocytopenia occurring in the context of an incidental monoclonal gammopathy, an increasingly common clinical scenario given the rising prevalence of MGUS with age. The authors explicitly caution against premature classification of thrombotic microangiopathy or antiphospholipid syndrome co-occurring with a monoclonal protein as MGTS absent direct causal evidence, urging instead the provisional label "thrombotic syndrome associated with M-protein" for these presentations[^5].

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

**Bone Health in Hemophilia and VWD — Novel Cross-Sectional Data.** A *Blood Advances* study by Citla-Sridhar et al. addresses an underexplored comorbidity axis in inherited bleeding disorders: skeletal health[^2]. In a three-group cross-sectional study of participants aged 5–45 years (persons with hemophilia [PwH], n=34; persons with von Willebrand disease [PwVWD], n=45; healthy controls, n=30), total bone mineral density (BMD) and bone mineral content (BMC) were assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, with trabecular/cortical parameters further characterized by peripheral quantitative computed tomography. PwVWD demonstrated significantly lower BMD z-scores compared with controls, while PwH showed a numerically similar but statistically nonsignificant trend, likely reflecting the smaller PwH sample size. Both PwH and PwVWD groups had significantly lower BMC than controls. Mechanistically, the study introduces simultaneous thrombin-plasmin (STP) generation assays as a novel correlate of bone health: in multivariable regression, higher thrombin generation was associated with higher BMD z-scores, whereas higher plasmin generation was associated with lower BMD z-scores, and STP parameters accounted for a greater proportion of variance in BMD than models incorporating only clinical factors or conventional bone turnover markers (serum procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide and C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen). An accompanying editorial by Machin frames these findings as hypothesis-generating evidence for a hemostasis-bone axis, calling explicitly for longitudinal and mechanistic follow-up studies to determine whether correcting coagulation/fibrinolytic imbalance could mitigate fracture risk in bleeding disorder populations[^3]. For NCKUH practitioners managing an aging hemophilia cohort — now living longer due to effective factor replacement and non-factor therapies — this data supports consideration of routine bone density surveillance in adult PwH and PwVWD, particularly those with a history of high plasmin/fibrinolytic activity or recurrent joint bleeding.

## VI. Von Willebrand Disease

**Bevacizumab for VWD-Associated Gastrointestinal Vascular Malformations.** An *AJH* observational cohort study by Ayad et al. evaluated systemic bevacizumab for severe bleeding from acquired gastrointestinal vascular malformations, a population that included patients with deficiencies of von Willebrand factor alongside idiopathic angiodysplasia and chronic liver disease[^6]. Among 32 patients (median age 75 years, 44% female) treated per a predefined institutional bevacizumab pathway, the composite Hematologic Support Score (integrating red-cell units transfused and elemental iron infused) improved from 15.04 red-cell-unit equivalents (95% CI, 11.16–21.80) in the 6 months pretreatment to 4.08 (95% CI, 4.00–8.00) during months 1–6 post-bevacizumab (p<0.001), with further improvement to 0.00 (95% CI, 0.00–5.40) during months 7–12 (p<0.001). Annualized hospitalization/emergency department visit rate fell from 3 to 1 per year (p=0.01), and median hemoglobin rose by 3.1 g/dL (p<0.001). Proteinuria and hypertension occurred in 25% and 16% of patients respectively, leading to bevacizumab discontinuation in 9%. While not a VWD-specific trial, this dataset is directly relevant to the subset of patients with VWD-related angiodysplastic bleeding refractory to standard hemostatic and replacement therapy, offering a targeted anti-angiogenic option beyond somatostatin analogs[^6]. The companion cross-sectional BMD study above also included von Willebrand disease as a primary cohort of interest[^2].

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

**Real-World Pediatric HSCT Outcomes in Transfusion-Dependent β-Thalassemia.** Complementing last week's comprehensive *AJH* review of the β-thalassemia clinical trial landscape[^4 — note: prior week], a new *AJH* report from Al-Jefri et al. presents a two-decade single-center experience of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in children with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia[^7]. While detailed outcome statistics were not available in the abstract cache, the study represents a substantial real-world dataset from a high-prevalence referral center and provides longitudinal context for benchmarking curative approaches (allogeneic HSCT) against the newer gene-addition and gene-editing therapies (betibeglogene autotemcel, exagamglogene autotemcel) discussed in the 2026 trials update[^4]. The updated *AJH* review by Musallam et al. reiterates that luspatercept and mitapivat have achieved clinically meaningful hemoglobin improvements and transfusion burden reduction and are now approved across multiple jurisdictions, while additional pyruvate kinase activators such as etavopivat remain in active clinical evaluation, and several fetal hemoglobin-induction and iron-metabolism-targeted approaches have failed to translate preclinical proof-of-concept into clinical benefit[^4]. The review explicitly flags pediatric access expansion and cost/accessibility barriers as unresolved priorities — directly relevant to Taiwan's NHI reimbursement planning as gene therapies mature.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

**Cost-Effectiveness of Ferritin-Based Screening in Pregnancy.** A *Blood Advances* cost-effectiveness analysis by Wang et al. addresses the absence of US guidelines for routine iron deficiency (ID) screening in pregnancy, noting that maternal ID affects nearly 85% of pregnancies globally by the third trimester[^8]. Using a modified societal perspective incorporating wages lost to infusion time, infusion administration costs, and annual hematology follow-up costs, the analysis compared ferritin thresholds of 30 μg/L, 15 μg/L, and no screening. Screening at 30 μg/L was the preferred strategy across extensive sensitivity analyses and all examined willingness-to-pay thresholds, yielding an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $34,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) (95% credible interval, $32,000–$36,200) versus no screening, and was favored in 100% of 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations. Although a US-focused pharmacoeconomic model, the findings support a physiologically informed (rather than purely diagnostic) ferritin threshold for second- and third-trimester screening, a framework potentially adaptable to obstetric-hematology co-management pathways at NCKUH given the comparable underrecognition of gestational ID in Asian populations.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

**Monoclonal Gammopathy of Thrombotic Significance — A New Diagnostic Construct.** Ho, Zanwar, Kumar, and Rajkumar propose in *AJH* the concept of monoclonal gammopathy of thrombotic significance (MGTS) as an extension of the monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance (MGCS) framework, analogous to monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance[^5]. The authors define MGTS as encompassing thrombotic disorders with definitive, mechanistically supported evidence that a monoclonal protein directly contributes to thrombosis, concluding that at present only monoclonal protein–induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis meets this threshold. Thrombotic microangiopathy and antiphospholipid syndrome occurring alongside a monoclonal protein are explicitly excluded from formal MGTS classification pending causal validation and are instead termed "thrombotic syndromes associated with M-proteins." This nosological clarification is clinically important for hematologists at NCKUH evaluating patients with unexplained thrombosis and incidental MGUS, as it discourages premature attribution of thrombosis to the paraprotein without mechanistic work-up, while formally validating a distinct entity for future diagnostic criteria development[^5].

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Taiwan's β-thalassemia carrier prevalence (~1–2% for β-globin variants, with regional clustering in southern Taiwan) and alpha-thalassemia carrier prevalence (~4–5%) continue to justify prioritized national screening and genetic counseling programs, and the newly highlighted two-decade pediatric HSCT experience from a high-prevalence Middle Eastern center provides a useful international benchmark for NCKUH's own transplant outcomes discussion as gene therapy access remains limited under current NHI reimbursement frameworks[^7],[^4]. For hemophilia and VWD populations followed at NCKUH's hemostasis clinic, this week's BMD/STP findings support consideration of incorporating dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry into routine long-term follow-up for adult patients, particularly those with recurrent hemarthrosis or elevated fibrinolytic activity, an area not currently standardized in Taiwan's chronic bleeding disorder care pathways[^2],[^3]. Iptacopan remains under NHI price negotiation for PNH in Taiwan; no new efficacy data beyond previously reported conference abstracts were available this week, and formal publication should be monitored for update in subsequent cycles. The bevacizumab pathway for angiodysplasia-related bleeding, while not yet a formal NHI-reimbursed indication in Taiwan, represents a therapeutic option meriting case-by-case special-review application for patients with VWD-associated refractory GI bleeding who have exhausted endoscopic and hormonal therapy options[^6].

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The bone health data in hemophilia and VWD represent the most novel mechanistic contribution this week, introducing thrombin-plasmin balance as a candidate biomarker of skeletal fragility in bleeding disorders and warranting consideration of BMD screening in NCKUH's adult hemostasis population[^2],[^3]. The formal MGTS nosology from the AJH review provides a practical diagnostic filter for hematologists confronting thrombosis with incidental monoclonal gammopathy, cautioning against over-attribution to the paraprotein absent mechanistic evidence[^5]. The bevacizumab cohort data offer a concrete, quantifiable therapeutic option for refractory GI angiodysplasia bleeding, including in the VWD subset, with a clear dose-response reduction in transfusion burden[^6]. The pregnancy iron-deficiency screening cost-effectiveness analysis, while US-centric, supports broader adoption of a 30 μg/L ferritin threshold that could inform obstetric-hematology screening protocols regionally[^8]. Finally, the formal publication of the McMaster ITP emergency bleed guideline solidifies a citable, algorithmic reference for NCKUH's emergency department and hematology on-call protocols[^1].

## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a *Blood* study by Stam et al. establishes that anti-HPA-1a antibodies in fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT) mechanistically block integrin αIIbβ3/αvβ3 activation through their Fab region in a concentration-dependent manner, and that the degree of integrin inhibition correlates with clinical disease severity — findings with immediate translational potential for prenatal risk stratification in FNAIT[^1]. Second, a companion *Blood Journal* paper by Waxmann et al. provides the first combined biochemical and functional evidence that anti-αvβ3 antibodies drive VEGFR2 fixation, phosphorylation, and endothelial sprouting impairment specifically in FNAIT cases complicated by intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), with blinded testing correctly classifying 6/6 ICH-positive and 4/4 ICH-negative cases, identifying disturbed angiogenesis as the pivotal mechanism of ICH in this condition[^2]. Third, a *Blood Journal* report by Morris et al. characterises FVIII-Aurora (FVIII-R571S), the first naturally occurring gain-of-function FVIII variant identified in a patient with recurrent thrombosis, demonstrating 10–20-fold higher FVIIIa affinity for FIXa and a 4–5-fold increase in procoagulant potency in murine tail-clip assays — defining a novel thrombophilic mechanism with implications for diagnostic interpretation of discordant one-stage versus chromogenic FVIII assay results[^3]. Fourth, a *Haematologica* post-hoc pooled analysis by Wang et al. (n = 3,337 participants with unprovoked VTE) demonstrates that on-treatment D-dimer level, measured during extended anticoagulation, predicts major bleeding risk in a graded fashion: patients in the highest D-dimer quartile had a 2.7-fold higher risk of major bleeding compared to the lowest quartile (adjusted HR 2.70; 95% CI 1.35–5.41; p = 0.005), simultaneously underscoring the dual role of D-dimer as both a recurrence and bleeding predictor in this challenging anticoagulation decision[^4]. Fifth, a *Blood Advances* systematic review and meta-analysis by Vrotniakaite-Bajerciene et al. (51 studies; n = 34,623 patients) quantifies recurrent VTE risk after anticoagulation cessation across all major thrombophilias, finding approximately 2–3-fold increased risk with antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, antithrombin deficiency, homozygous factor V Leiden (FVL), and compound heterozygous FVL/prothrombin G20210A mutations, versus a more modest 1.5-fold increased risk for isolated heterozygous FVL or prothrombin mutation — providing a meta-analytic framework directly applicable to secondary VTE prevention counselling[^5].

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

Two highly complementary papers published this week substantially advance understanding of FVIII biology, one defining a novel prothrombotic gain-of-function variant and the other providing real-world evidence for efanesoctocog alfa (efa) in complex hemophilia A patients.

The first paper, by Morris et al. in *Blood Journal*, characterises FVIII-Aurora (FVIII-R571S), the first naturally occurring enhanced-potency FVIII variant, identified in a patient with recurrent thrombosis and early mortality[^3]. Recombinant FVIII-R571S demonstrated a 6-fold increase in one-stage assay activity compared to wild-type FVIII, while chromogenic substrate assay activity was comparable — a discrepancy explained by the finding that activated FVIIIa-R571S possesses 10–20-fold higher affinity for FIXa. Critically, the chromogenic assay is insensitive to differences in FVIIIa-FIXa binding affinity, which explains the assay divergence and underlines the risk of under-detecting a potent gain-of-function variant using chromogenic assays alone. FVIIIa-R571S also exhibited reduced A2-domain dissociation and functional resistance to activated protein C (APC) mediated inactivation in the presence of FIXa, conferring protection of the FVIIIa-FIXa complex from physiological downregulation. In haemophilia A mice, FVIII-R571S showed a 4–5-fold increase in procoagulant potency versus wild-type in the tail-clip assay, and promoted significantly increased platelet and fibrin accumulation in a thrombosis model at equivalent antigen concentrations. The clinical implications extend beyond the individual case: any patient presenting with discordant high one-stage and normal chromogenic FVIII activity, unexplained recurrent thrombosis, and a family history of thrombophilia should prompt consideration of this variant in the differential diagnosis. Furthermore, the observation that standard chromogenic assays fail to detect the pathological potency increase has direct implications for haemostasis laboratories.

The second paper, the ECHO study by Rauch et al. in *Blood Advances*, provides a real-world evaluation of efanesoctocog alfa within the French compassionate access framework, enrolling 50 hemophilia A patients (84% severe; median age 31 years), including 9 with inhibitor history[^9]. Of particular clinical interest is that 46% of patients transitioned from emicizumab due to persistent breakthrough bleeding (88%) and complex multifactorial scenarios (76%), demonstrating a real-world treatment gap even with a subcutaneous non-factor prophylaxis option. Over a median 9-month follow-up, weekly efa prophylaxis resulted in an 89% reduction in treated bleeds (p < 0.001) and an 85% reduction in joint bleeds (p < 0.001) across the total population, including among patients previously on emicizumab. No new-onset inhibitors were observed. An important laboratory monitoring concern was identified: local one-stage assays yielded a median day-7 trough of 14 IU/dL, compared to a centralized drug-calibrated bovine chromogenic substrate assay median of 7 IU/dL (p < 0.001), a statistically and clinically significant discrepancy observed across various local reagents. This monitoring discrepancy — directly relevant to NCKUH clinical laboratory practice — necessitates caution when interpreting local one-stage assay results for efa-treated patients and underlines the need for drug-calibrated chromogenic assay availability. Complementarily, a *Haematologica* letter by Erard et al. on short-term emicizumab use in acquired hemophilia A was also published this week, though complete data were not available in the current feed[^10].

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

A comprehensive *BJH* review by Joshi et al. maps the emerging landscape of gene therapies for inherited non-haemoglobinopathy erythroid disorders — including Diamond–Blackfan anaemia, pyruvate kinase deficiency, X-linked sideroblastic anaemia, congenital erythropoietic porphyria, and congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia — and synthesises the cellular engineering approaches, conditioning strategies, and biological themes shared across these rare conditions[^11]. Although focused on non-haemoglobinopathy disorders, the review explicitly builds on the translational lessons from FDA-approved gene therapy for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia and provides a framework for accelerating clinical translation by leveraging convergent biology. For NCKUH hematologists managing beta-thalassaemia patients, the review's discussion of conditioning optimisation, HSPC collection methods, and shared lentiviral vector design principles is directly applicable to interpreting emerging data from the haemoglobinopathy gene therapy programmes in Taiwan's patient population.

A separate *Blood Advances* report by Prueksapraopong et al. on lentiviral vector gene therapy for sickle cell disease (the Lenti/G-βAS3-FB protocol; NCT02247843) enrolled 4 adults with severe SCD[^12]. Three patients treated with an optimised protocol (including plerixafor mobilisation, precollection erythrocytapheresis, and enhanced lentiviral vector) achieved peak granulocyte vector copy numbers of approximately 0.5–2.0 and persistent HbAS3 expression, with 2 becoming transfusion independent. One earlier patient treated with the original protocol had a peak VCN of only 0.035 and undetectable HbAS3. No insertional oncogenesis was observed. Although this specific vector will not be pursued further by the investigators, the protocol optimisation insights are generalisable and inform best practices for similar programmes in Asia.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, ATP11C)

Three papers this week substantially expand the diagnostic and biological landscape of rare inherited red cell disorders.

First, a *BJH* paper by Marin et al. applies whole-genome sequencing and functional splicing assays to characterise deep intronic *ANK1* variants causing pseudo-exon inclusion in hereditary spherocytosis (HS), providing a mechanism for the diagnostic gap in HS patients with no coding variant identified by conventional panels[^13]. The inclusion of pseudo-exon-creating intronic variants in *ANK1* explains a subset of previously genetically undiagnosed HS cases and directly argues for incorporating whole-genome or deep intronic sequencing strategies into genetic workup for clinically diagnosed HS — particularly relevant to Taiwan where HS is not uncommon and genetic confirmation has both diagnostic and family counselling utility.

Second, a *Blood Advances* paper by D'Onofrio et al. reports 10 individuals from 7 unrelated families harbouring 6 novel variants in *ATP11C*, the gene encoding the major phosphatidylserine flippase in human red blood cells, substantially expanding the clinical and phenotypic spectrum of this recently described X-linked congenital hemolytic anemia[^14]. Clinical heterogeneity was striking: the first functionally validated variant (p.R467C) presented in a male neonate with mild haemolytic anaemia; the second (c.2226-1G>C) was identified in a 53-year-old male originally suspected of hereditary hemochromatosis; and the third (p.D609V) was detected in a 68-year-old female with mild anaemia who had skewed X-chromosome inactivation making the heterozygous pathogenic variant clinically manifest. Functional studies confirmed reduced ATP11C protein expression and decreased flippase activity both in vitro and in ex vivo red blood cells. Importantly, the association of ATP11C loss-of-function with adult-onset disease and hepatic iron overload — mimicking hereditary hemochromatosis — means this gene should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained haemolytic anaemia with iron overload across all age groups.

Third, a mechanistically important paper by Xie et al. in *Blood Journal* identifies a gut microbiome–erythropoiesis axis in aging-related anaemia[^15]. Using both human and murine cohorts, the authors show that ageing markedly elevates plasma phenylacetic acid (PAA) levels, driven by increased abundance of *Odoribacter splanchnicus* in the ageing gut microbiome. PAA promotes a novel histone lysine phenylacetylation (Kpa) post-translational modification via the acetyltransferase HBO1, which increases chromatin accessibility at the *GATA2* promoter, disrupts the GATA switch, and blocks erythroid differentiation of haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. Rifaximin selectively reduced *O. splanchnicus* and circulating PAA, alleviating anaemia in aged mice, and dietary phenylalanine restriction produced similar effects. The HBO1 inhibitor WM-3835 restored erythropoiesis by reversing histone Kpa in vivo. While still at the translational research stage, this work identifies a modifiable gut–epigenetic–erythropoiesis pathway that could, in principle, be targeted to address the highly prevalent but mechanistically poorly understood anaemia of ageing.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

A *BJH* study by Addo et al. (n = 907 adult blood donors) provides a rigorous physiologically based calibration of serum hepcidin and ferritin thresholds for detecting iron-deficient erythropoiesis using restricted cubic spline models against five erythropoietic indicators (haemoglobin, soluble transferrin receptor [sTfR], erythrocyte zinc protoporphyrin [ZPP], reticulocyte haemoglobin content, and mean corpuscular volume)[^16]. Serum hepcidin and ferritin were strongly correlated (Pearson's r = 0.69, p < 0.001 in females; r = 0.65, p < 0.001 in males). The derived hepcidin thresholds for iron deficiency were 13.9 µg/L for females aged 18–49 years, 24.8 µg/L for females aged 50–75 years, and 28.8 µg/L for all male donors aged 18–75 years; the corresponding ferritin thresholds were 25.4, 30.7, and 32.5 µg/L respectively. Among donors younger than 50 years, iron deficiency thresholds were lower in women than in men but converged after age 50. These data provide an empirically derived, sex- and age-stratified reference framework for iron deficiency assessment in blood donors — directly relevant to Taiwan Blood Services practices, where donor iron status monitoring protocols are under ongoing evaluation.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

Three highly complementary publications this week collectively redefine the clinical management of extended anticoagulation and thrombophilia-associated recurrent VTE.

The first, a *Haematologica* post-hoc pooled analysis by Wang et al. of the BLEEDRISK and REVERSE-2 cohorts (n = 3,337 participants with unprovoked VTE who continued anticoagulation after at least 3 months of initial treatment), quantifies major bleeding risk stratified by on-treatment D-dimer quartiles[^4]. Incidence rates of anticoagulant-associated major bleeding from the lowest to highest D-dimer quartile were 0.45, 0.91, 1.43, and 2.20 events per 100 person-years respectively. After adjustment for age, sex, and anticoagulant type, individuals in the highest D-dimer quartile had a 2.7-fold higher major bleeding risk compared to the lowest quartile (adjusted HR 2.70; 95% CI 1.35–5.41; p = 0.005). Intracranial haemorrhage and fatal bleeding did not differ significantly across quartiles. These findings create a clinically actionable paradox: the same biomarker (elevated on-treatment D-dimer) that predicts recurrent VTE after anticoagulation cessation also independently predicts major bleeding during extended anticoagulation, meaning that elevated on-treatment D-dimer alone cannot unidirectionally guide treatment duration. The authors correctly conclude that additional risk stratification tools incorporating both bleeding and recurrence predictors are urgently needed for this population.

The second, a *Blood Advances* systematic review and meta-analysis by Vrotniakaite-Bajerciene et al. (51 studies; n = 34,623 patients; 42.3% female; 33.5% with unprovoked index VTE) comprehensively quantifies recurrent VTE risk after anticoagulation cessation across the full spectrum of hereditary and acquired thrombophilias[^5]. Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS), antithrombin deficiency, homozygous FVL, and compound heterozygous FVL/prothrombin G20210A mutation were each associated with an approximately 2–3-fold increased recurrent VTE risk. Heterozygous FVL or prothrombin G20210A mutation in isolation conferred a more modest approximately 1.5-fold increased risk. Evidence regarding protein S and protein C deficiency remained inconclusive, with predominantly moderate certainty of evidence assessed by GRADE criteria. These data provide a stratified risk matrix applicable to secondary VTE prevention counselling in outpatient anticoagulation clinics, directly relevant to NCKUH thrombophilia management practice.

The third, a mechanistic *Blood* paper by Yang et al. identifies the oxidised state of protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) as the dominant driver of thrombosis in oxidative stress conditions[^17]. Using pharmacological (LOC14, which inhibits PDI reductase activity and induces PDI oxidation) and genetic approaches, the authors demonstrate that LOC14 promoted thrombus formation in ferric chloride–exposed arteries and enhanced laser injury–induced platelet accumulation and fibrin formation in cremaster arterioles — effects reversed by blocking anti-PDI antibodies. The sulfenylation-mediated oxidation mechanism of PDI was characterised using a series of PDI mutants (C53A, C56A, R120D, T101A), demonstrating that the sulfenylation mechanism resembles hydrogen peroxide reduction by peroxiredoxins. Critically, in mice with genetic deletion of glutathione peroxidase 3 or following infusion of oxidised low-density lipoproteins — both models of systemic oxidative stress — the sulfenylation-impaired R120D PDI mutant was unable to fully restore thrombus formation, while wild-type PDI could. A companion *Blood* editorial by Hollenhorst provides mechanistic context[^18]. These findings establish a discrete PDI-mediated oxidant-thrombosis axis that is specifically amplified in oxidative stress states such as atherosclerosis, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome, suggesting PDI oxidase activity as a potential antithrombotic target in cardiovascular-risk thrombosis.

A fourth paper, by Yakusheva et al. in *Blood*, identifies fibrin's role as a thrombin sink that maintains a thrombotic remnant in an inactive "stillness phase" at the end stage of non-occlusive thrombus formation[^19]. Using intravital microscopy, the authors showed that platelet attachment to fibrin-rich thrombi ceased after partial disaggregation. In vivo, treatment with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) during the stillness phase released active thrombin, leading to unexpected thrombus regrowth — a finding with direct clinical implications for thrombolysis timing and its potential to paradoxically re-activate a quiescent thrombus. FgaEK mice, unable to form fibrin clots, exhibited repeated cycles of thrombus growth and detachment with continuous thrombin release, confirming fibrin's indispensable role in achieving a stable thrombotic end state.

## XII. FNAIT — Fetal/Neonatal Alloimmune Thrombocytopenia

Two mechanistically complementary papers published this week substantially advance understanding of FNAIT pathophysiology, particularly with respect to the still-unpredictable complication of intracranial haemorrhage.

Stam et al., writing in *Blood*, systematically characterise how maternal anti-HPA-1a antibodies impair integrin αIIbβ3 and αvβ3 activation using a panel of recombinant anti-HPA-1a antibodies and a retrospective cohort of FNAIT samples associated with mild or severe disease[^1]. The antibodies inhibited binding of both integrins to their ligands (vitronectin, fibronectin, fibrinogen) and impaired cell adhesion in a concentration-dependent, Fab-mediated, Fc-independent manner. When integrins were forced into a constitutively extended (active) conformation, the inhibitory effect was abolished, confirming that anti-HPA-1a antibodies act by preventing integrin activation rather than by blocking ligand-binding sites on already-active integrins. Critically, the degree of integrin inhibition correlated with disease severity in the clinical cohort, providing the first mechanistic link between antibody functional activity and clinical outcome that could be exploited as a prenatal severity biomarker. A companion *Blood* editorial by Wu and Ni contextualises these findings in the broader question of whether epitope specificity dictates FNAIT severity[^20].

Waxmann et al., in *Blood Journal*, address specifically the mechanism of ICH in FNAIT by characterising the subset of anti-HPA-1a antibodies with anti-αvβ3 specificity on fetal endothelial cells[^2]. Analysis of human FNAIT sera demonstrated that anti-αvβ3-containing sera induced VEGFR2 fixation on the endothelial cell surface, enhanced VEGFR2 phosphorylation, elevated reactive oxygen species production, and impaired endothelial sprouting — a functional angiogenesis defect independent of the degree of platelet-mediated thrombocytopenia. In a murine model, only offspring of β3 knockout mice immunised with wild-type platelets who developed anti-αvβ3 antibodies developed ICH — not those with exclusively anti-αIIbβ3 antibodies despite equivalent thrombocytopenia. Blinded testing of human FNAIT samples using the combined biochemical and functional assay panel correctly classified 6/6 ICH-positive and 4/4 ICH-negative cases, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy for the most severe FNAIT complication. Together, these two papers collectively reframe FNAIT pathophysiology: platelet destruction mediates thrombocytopenia, but it is the anti-αvβ3 endothelial impairment of angiogenesis and vascular integrity that drives ICH, explaining why thrombocytopenia severity alone is a poor predictor of intracranial bleeding risk.

## XIV. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings from this week's report carry particular relevance to clinical practice at NCKUH and affiliated institutions in Taiwan. The FNAIT papers by Stam et al. and Waxmann et al. have immediate implications for obstetric hematology consultations at NCKUH: given that anti-HPA-1a alloimmunization (HPA-1 antigen incompatibility) is less prevalent in East Asian populations compared to Caucasians — where HPA-1b (Pro33) allele frequency is lower in Chinese Han populations — the more relevant antigen mismatches in Taiwanese FNAIT cases may differ (including HPA-3, HPA-5, and HPA-21 specificities). However, the mechanistic framework of integrin inhibition severity correlating with clinical outcome is likely generalizable, and the proposed anti-αvβ3 ICH-risk assay represents a prenatal tool that should be evaluated prospectively in East Asian FNAIT cohorts.

For hemophilia A patients, NCKUH's access to efanesoctocog alfa under the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration (TFDA) approval framework (approved in Taiwan) is directly informed by the ECHO real-world data[^9]. The laboratory monitoring discrepancy between local one-stage assays and drug-calibrated chromogenic substrate assays identified in ECHO (median 14 vs. 7 IU/dL respectively; p < 0.001) is immediately actionable: NCKUH haematology and coagulation laboratories should verify that efa monitoring uses drug-calibrated chromogenic or recombinant FIXa-based assays rather than standard one-stage clot-based assays to avoid systematic overestimation of trough FVIII levels and consequent under-dosing of breakthrough bleed-exposed patients.

Regarding VTE management, the D-dimer paradox demonstrated by Wang et al. — where elevated on-treatment D-dimer simultaneously predicts both recurrence risk (if anticoagulation stopped) and major bleeding risk (if anticoagulation continued) — is directly applicable to the common clinical dilemma of anticoagulation duration after a first unprovoked VTE event, seen frequently in the NCKUH outpatient anticoagulation clinic. The VTE thrombophilia meta-analysis by Vrotniakaite-Bajerciene et al.[^5] confirms that protein C and protein S deficiency data remain inconclusive — relevant because these deficiencies are disproportionately represented in East Asian VTE cohorts compared to FVL (which is uncommon in Han Chinese populations, where FVL frequency approaches 0–1%). Clinicians at NCKUH should apply thrombophilia recurrence risk estimates differentially, recognising that APS and antithrombin deficiency carry the highest evidence-based recurrence hazard in the Asian thrombophilia context.

The *BJH* deep intronic *ANK1* paper[^13] has direct utility for the NCKUH Rare Red Cell Disorders program: it supports incorporating whole-genome sequencing or targeted deep intronic panels into workup for hereditary spherocytosis patients without a coding-region explanatory variant, particularly given Taiwan's established programme for rare inherited haematological diagnoses. The *Blood Advances* ATP11C report[^14] similarly broadens the diagnostic differential for unexplained haemolytic anaemia with iron overload in Taiwanese patients, where hereditary hemochromatosis (HFE-related) is rare but ATP11C-related disease may be under-recognised.

Taiwan's NHI currently covers emicizumab for hemophilia A with inhibitors and non-inhibitor severe hemophilia A, eculizumab for PNH, eltrombopag and romiplostim for ITP, and deferasirox for transfusion-dependent thalassaemia. Efanesoctocog alfa received TFDA approval and is under NHI coverage review. Iptacopan for PNH is not yet NHI-listed in Taiwan as of this report date.

## XV. Key Takeaways

This week's most actionable findings can be distilled into four practice-shaping messages. First, FNAIT pathophysiology is now bipartite: anti-HPA-1a antibodies mediate thrombocytopenia through Fab-dependent integrin activation blockade (severity correlating with degree of blockade), while ICH risk specifically maps to anti-αvβ3 antibodies disrupting fetal endothelial VEGFR2 signalling and angiogenesis — creating a mechanistic basis for a prenatal ICH-risk assay. Second, FVIII-Aurora demonstrates that standard chromogenic FVIII assays can fail to detect clinically dangerous gain-of-function variants, raising diagnostic awareness for laboratories encountering discordant one-stage versus chromogenic FVIII results in patients with unexplained recurrent thrombosis. Third, the D-dimer paradox in extended anticoagulation — where the same elevated marker simultaneously predicts recurrence if anticoagulation stops and bleeding if it continues — quantitatively reframes the risk-benefit conversation with patients about indefinite anticoagulation after unprovoked VTE. Fourth, the thrombophilia meta-analysis provides a graded recurrence risk hierarchy (APS/AT deficiency/homozygous FVL ~2–3-fold; heterozygous FVL/PTM ~1.5-fold) that should be integrated into thrombophilia clinic counselling, noting the critical caveat that FVL is rare in East Asian populations and protein C/S deficiency evidence remains inconclusive.

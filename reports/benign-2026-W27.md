## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a landmark *Blood* translational study by Wagner et al. (n = 357 patients, validated in >20,000 from TriNetX) demonstrates that ferric carboxymaltose (FCM), but not ferric derisomaltose (FDI), independently increases incident fracture and osteomalacia risk, revealing a mechanistic basis in FCM-mediated suppression of osteoblast collagen and ossification gene expression associated with higher bone iron concentrations — a finding with immediate prescribing implications for Taiwanese patients with iron deficiency anemia who receive repeated IV iron infusions[^1]. Second, two complementary *Blood* papers this week substantially reframe the pathogenesis and diagnosis of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT): Zhou et al. identify a prevalent, previously unrecognised subset of ELISA-negative, PEA-positive platelet-activating antibodies that are PF4-dependent and thrombocytopenic in a humanised mouse model, comprising 65% ± 19% of total platelet-activating IgG activity in confirmed HIT patients[^2], while Bissola et al. engineer single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) from the HIT-like monoclonal antibody KKO to selectively inhibit pathogenic but not nonpathogenic anti-PF4/heparin antibodies in patient sera[^3]. Third, a *Blood* mechanistic study by Field et al. establishes that PF4 is a central immunothrombotic scaffold in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), demonstrating that PF4 bridges β2-glycoprotein I to neutrophil extracellular traps and that three antigenically distinct anti-hPF4 monoclonal antibodies can block thrombosis in APS murine models — providing a conceptual framework analogous to HIT and proposing non-anticoagulant therapeutic strategies[^4]. Fourth, a *Haematologica* real-world report by La Mura et al. describes biopsy-proven immune-mediated hepatitis as a mechanism of alanine aminotransferase elevation after valoctocogene roxaparvovec gene therapy for severe haemophilia A, demonstrating that a personalised prednisone schedule based on liver biopsy findings normalised ALT with preservation of transgene expression (median chromogenic FVIII activity 83% [range 12–206%] at end of follow-up)[^5]. Fifth, a *Blood* study by Sternberg et al. demonstrates in both mice and 23 SPK-8011 gene therapy trial participants that the one-stage assay (OSA) — but not the chromogenic substrate assay (CSA) — captures enhanced thrombin-mediated activation of AAV-derived FVIII-SQ, and that OSA activity better correlates with in vivo haemostatic function and trends toward better prediction of annualised bleeding rate, directly informing how to measure and interpret FVIII levels in gene therapy recipients[^6].

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

This week brings two important contributions to haemophilia A biology and gene therapy monitoring. The first, from Sternberg et al. published in *Blood*, addresses a persistent clinical question: which laboratory assay best reflects haemostatic function in patients who have received AAV-based FVIII-SQ gene therapy? Using data from mice and 23 participants in the SPK-8011 gene therapy trial (representing 99 cumulative patient-years), the authors demonstrate that AAV-derived FVIII-SQ exhibits enhanced thrombin-mediated activation compared with recombinant FVIII-SQ[^6]. This enhanced activation is detectable by OSA but not by CSA, explaining the previously puzzling 1.5- to 2-fold OSA-to-CSA discrepancy consistently observed in gene therapy recipients. Importantly, in vivo haemostatic function in haemophilic mice correlated better with OSA values than with CSA values, and negative binomial regression of the 23 trial participants showed a trend toward superior prediction of annualised bleeding rate by OSA (p not reported as significant but directionally consistent). The authors further demonstrate that the OSA–CSA discrepancy persists in mice in the absence of von Willebrand factor, arguing against altered vWF interactions as the cause, and that AAV-FVIII-SQ retains normal cofactor function and A2-domain stability in the intrinsic tenase complex. The practical implication for NCKUH and other centres monitoring gene therapy recipients is clear: OSA — the standard haemophilia laboratory assay — captures the clinically relevant FVIII activity in this setting and should be the primary monitoring tool, with CSA understood to systematically underestimate functional activity.

The second contribution, a *Haematologica* case series from La Mura et al. (n = 5 patients treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec), characterises biopsy-proven immune-mediated hepatitis as the histopathological substrate of ALT elevation in severe haemophilia A gene therapy recipients[^5]. Four of five patients (80%) had ALT >1.5× baseline, but two with transaminase above the upper limit of normal and declining transgene expression underwent transjugular liver biopsy before prednisone initiation, which revealed acute immune-mediated hepatitis and abnormal enlargement of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum. Immunophenotyping showed high T-cytotoxic and low T-regulatory activity during acute hepatitis. Following a personalised oral prednisone schedule guided by biopsy findings, ALT normalised with preservation of transgene expression in both cases. Median FVIII activity at end of follow-up was 83% by chromogenic assay (range 12–206%), with wide variability regardless of prednisone exposure. The data suggest that biopsy-guided, personalised immunosuppression — rather than empirical corticosteroid protocols triggered by ALT thresholds alone — may optimise the balance between immune control and transgene preservation. This report is relevant to Taiwanese haemophilia centres as valoctocogene roxaparvovec (BioMarin) received EMA approval in 2022 and is under regulatory consideration in other jurisdictions.

A third mechanistic paper from Nardini et al. in *Haematologica* examines sialylated immunodominant peptides as a strategy to modulate T-cell responses to FVIII in haemophilia A inhibitor patients; the full abstract was not available at time of writing, but the work is noted as potentially relevant to inhibitor immunotolerance strategies[^8].

Additionally, a *Blood Advances* study by Furukawa et al. investigated maternal-fetal transfer of emicizumab in a pregnant mouse model; the abstract was not available in full, but this work is noted for its relevance to management of emicizumab-treated women of childbearing age in Taiwan's NHI-covered haemophilia programme[^9].

## VIII. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

A clinically informative retrospective analysis from Yan et al. published in *Haematologica* this week examines 1,404 patients with chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) and the prognostic impact of CLL-associated autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) across treatment eras[^11]. Although the primary focus is oncological, the data provide important insights into warm AIHA pathophysiology and the modifying effect of targeted therapies that are highly relevant to non-malignant haematologists managing AIHA in the context of CLL. The incidence of AIHA in this CLL cohort was 10.4%, with 69.2% classified as warm-antibody AIHA (wAIHA). Patients with CLL-associated AIHA were characterised by male predominance, advanced disease stage, IGHV4-34 usage, and higher frequency of DNMT3A mutations (particularly in wAIHA), while MYD88 mutations were enriched in cold-antibody AIHA — suggesting genetically distinct pathways to the two AIHA subtypes. Although AIHA conferred a significantly adverse prognostic impact on CLL outcomes, this effect was markedly attenuated in patients receiving targeted therapies (BTK inhibitors and venetoclax-based regimens). Notably, unmutated IGHV status predicted inferior outcomes in the overall CLL cohort but not among patients with AIHA, highlighting that AIHA and IGHV status are not merely co-linear adverse features and may identify overlapping but distinct high-risk biological subgroups. These findings support routine AIHA screening in high-risk CLL patients at NCKUH and underscore the value of early initiation of targeted therapy to mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of AIHA.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis)

A *Blood Advances* prospective clinical trial by Yee et al. (NCT04426591) provides new data on transfused RBC survival in sickle cell disease using biotin-labelling in 20 recipients over 22 transfusion episodes (49 units)[^10]. Median post-transfusion RBC recovery was 97.3% (range 79.4–112.7%) at 24 hours, 80% (range 35–105%) at 28 days, and 21% (range 2–48%) at 90 days, with a median time to 50% recovery of 60 days (range 23–85 days). In multivariate mixed-effects analysis, recipient spleen volume (p < 0.05) and donor G6PD deficiency or α-thalassemia trait were independently associated with decreased biotinylated RBC survival, while recipient RBC alloimmunisation showed a non-significant association. This study has direct Taiwan-relevant implications: given the high prevalence of G6PD deficiency (approximately 3% of males in Taiwan) and α-thalassemia trait (~4–5% of the population), blood bank screening of donor units for these traits may be relevant to optimise transfusion efficacy in patients with sickle cell disease or other haemoglobinopathies receiving chronic transfusion therapy in Taiwan, and warrants future prospective evaluation in this specific population.

A comprehensive *BJH* review by Twine and Rees on methaemoglobinaemia provides an updated clinical management framework, covering the pathophysiology of all three major aetiologies — Haemoglobin-M gene mutations, inherited cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency, and oxidising agent exposure — and reviewing treatment options including IV methylene blue (first-line for symptomatic cases), high-dose vitamin C, exchange transfusion, and hyperbaric oxygen[^12]. The review highlights emerging contributions from recreational and non-prescribed exposures and provides practical guidance on the misleading normality of arterial blood gas pO₂ and SpO₂ in this setting. This is a useful reference resource for emergency and general haematology practice at NCKUH.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

This week's most practice-changing finding for non-malignant haematology is the *Blood* study by Wagner et al. comparing the skeletal safety of two widely used IV iron formulations — ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) and ferric derisomaltose (FDI) — in a cohort of 357 patients with iron deficiency anaemia and in a validation cohort of >20,000 patients from the TriNetX database[^1]. The primary clinical finding is that FCM treatment was independently associated with a significantly higher fracture risk compared with FDI in the TriNetX analysis. In the smaller clinical cohort, patients treated with FCM reported a significantly higher rate of incident osteomalacia or fracture than those treated with FDI. The mechanistic investigation — conducted in a mouse model of IDA, an osteocyte cell model, and biochemically — demonstrates that FCM (but not FDI) causes markedly higher bone iron concentrations, detectable as iron-loaded vesicles in osteoblasts and early osteocytes by electron microscopy. FCM, but not FDI, suppressed the expression of collagen and ossification genes and inhibited the binding of dentin matrix protein 1 (DMP1) to αVβ3-integrin on osteocytes, a pathway linked to impaired mineralisation and elevated intact fibroblast growth factor 23 (iFGF23) — the mechanism underlying FCM-associated hypophosphataemia. This study is accompanied by an editorial commentary from Finberg and Auerbach in the same issue[^13].

The clinical implications are significant. FCM (Ferinject®) is currently the most commonly prescribed high-dose single-infusion IV iron in many Taiwanese centres because it allows rapid iron repletion in one or two infusions. FDI (Monofer®), which causes hypophosphataemia far less frequently and now demonstrates a superior bone safety profile in both clinical and mechanistic data, should be considered the preferred formulation for patients who require repeated IV iron infusions — particularly those with pre-existing osteopenia, osteomalacia risk factors, or who are on long-term iron therapy for inflammatory bowel disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease. Haematologists at NCKUH prescribing IV iron should review current protocols in light of these data.

A separate *Blood* translational study by Dunaway et al. investigates a novel vascular mechanism linking iron deficiency to endothelial nitric oxide (NO) signalling, demonstrating that IDA in female mice increases arterial NO signalling through a reduction in heme-bound (holo) endothelial α-globin, which normally scavenges NO in resistance artery endothelium[^14]. Iron repletion with iron dextran rescued NO signalling to control levels, and endothelial-specific α-globin knockout mice failed to respond to iron repletion, confirming the requirement for α-globin in this regulatory axis. Notably, male mice showed no change in NO signalling despite equivalent anaemia, attributed to preserved holo-α-globin. While these findings are preclinical and mechanistic, they provide a new framework for understanding the cardiovascular consequences of IDA — particularly the sex-specific vasodilatory response — and may be relevant to the cardiovascular phenotype of iron deficiency in women.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

**Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia — New Pathogenic Antibody Subset.** Two highly complementary *Blood* papers this week substantially reframe HIT pathophysiology and diagnostics. Zhou et al. identify a prevalent but previously unrecognised antibody population in HIT: IgG antibodies that are negative by standard PF4/heparin ELISA (ELISA−) yet activate platelets in the PF4-dependent P-selectin expression assay (PEA+)[^2]. In 11 patients with clinically confirmed HIT who tested positive in both ELISA and platelet activation assays, ELISA−PEA+ antibodies accounted for 65% ± 19% of total platelet-activating IgG activity and co-existed with conventional ELISA+PEA+ antibodies. Single-cell cloning from 7 HIT patients identified 23 PEA+ antibody-producing B-cell clones, of which 17 were ELISA−, numerically outnumbering ELISA+ clones. Critically, these antibodies required exogenous PF4 for platelet binding and activation, were inhibited by FcγRIIA blockade and high-dose heparin, and induced thrombocytopenia in a humanised mouse model of HIT. Despite binding PF4 on platelets, they showed no appreciable binding to PF4/heparin complexes in standard ELISA format, explaining their diagnostic invisibility. The clinical implications are profound: a substantial fraction of true HIT cases may currently be misclassified as ELISA-negative, thereby delaying diagnosis and anticoagulation. This provides a potential explanation for clinically compelling HIT with negative screening ELISAs — a scenario encountered at every major centre including NCKUH.

Bissola et al., also in *Blood*, approach the complementary diagnostic challenge of distinguishing pathogenic from the more common nonpathogenic anti-PF4/heparin antibodies that cause false-positive ELISA results[^3]. The group engineered scFv variants derived from the HIT-like murine monoclonal antibody KKO and demonstrated that five candidate scFvs selectively inhibited the binding of pathogenic anti-PF4/heparin HIT antibodies — and prevented platelet activation in some patient sera in the serotonin release assay — without perturbing nonpathogenic antibody binding. These scFvs exploit the fact that pathogenic HIT antibodies share an overlapping, heparin-dependent binding site on PF4 that can be competitively blocked. Together, these two papers from different angles converge on the conclusion that current PF4/heparin ELISA-based HIT diagnostics capture only a subset of the relevant immunological events, and that next-generation functional assays and epitope-specific blocking tools may be needed for definitive diagnosis.

**Antiphospholipid Syndrome — PF4 as Central Scaffold.** Field et al. in *Blood* present mechanistic evidence that PF4 plays a central, previously underappreciated role in APS immunothrombosis[^4]. The study demonstrates that PF4 binds β2-glycoprotein I (β2GPI), enhancing recognition of β2GPI by APS autoantibodies, and that NETs released in a microfluidic system bind β2GPI only in the presence of PF4, forming multimolecular APS antigenic targets. In a whole-blood microfluidic injury model, APS antibody binding to thrombi required PF4, and thrombus size was reduced by ADAMTS13 or DNase1 infusion. Using wildtype, hPF4-transgenic, FcγRIIA-expressing, and PF4-knockout murine APS models, the authors demonstrate that PF4-knockout mice fail to develop neutrophil rolling and thrombosis following laser injury to cremaster microvessels, despite harbouring equivalent circulating APS antibodies. Three antigenically distinct anti-hPF4 monoclonal antibodies blocked thrombosis in vitro and in vivo without requiring anticoagulation. This work establishes mechanistic parallels between APS and other PF4-centric immunothrombotic disorders (HIT, vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis), potentially unifying the field conceptually and opening the possibility that non-anticoagulant anti-PF4-targeted strategies could complement or eventually partially replace conventional anticoagulation in selected APS patients.

**Isolated Distal DVT — Post-Treatment Recurrence Risk.** A post-hoc analysis of the RIDTS trial by Potere et al. in *Haematologica* provides contemporary recurrence data for isolated distal deep vein thrombosis (IDDVT) after 6 versus 12 weeks of rivaroxaban in patients without cancer (n = 398)[^15]. Over a median of 6.2 months after anticoagulation cessation, 61 participants (15.3%) experienced recurrent VTE: 77% were recurrent IDDVT and 23% were proximal DVT or pulmonary embolism. The 6-month incidence rate for any recurrence was 15.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI 11.0–22.7) and 24.1 (95% CI 16.1–36.4) after unprovoked IDDVT. In multivariable analysis, shorter anticoagulation duration (HR 1.8; 95% CI 1.1–3.0), diabetes (HR 2.5; 95% CI 1.1–5.3), and unprovoked IDDVT (HR 3.2; 95% CI 1.7–5.7) were independent predictors of recurrence. Recurrence rates were substantially higher in the 6-week versus 12-week rivaroxaban arm during the first 6 months (24.4 vs 7.5 per 100 person-years). These data support extending anticoagulation to at least 12 weeks for unprovoked IDDVT, particularly in patients with diabetes, and have direct relevance to thrombosis practice at NCKUH where decisions about anticoagulation duration for distal DVT are common.

**Congenital Factor XII Deficiency — Clinical Heterogeneity.** A *BJH* pedigree analysis by Qu et al. characterises five unrelated families sharing an identical F12 frameshift mutation (c.303_304delCA, p.H101Qfs*36) yet displaying markedly discordant clinical phenotypes ranging from asymptomatic to thrombotic or haemorrhagic[^16]. In Pedigree A, thrombotic events were documented in 4 of 6 mutation carriers and 4 of 13 non-carriers, with ApoE and BTN2A1 variants consistently co-segregating with thrombosis. This study reinforces the prevailing view that FXII deficiency does not independently drive pathological coagulation but may potentiate susceptibility in the presence of additional genetic or acquired risk factors. The practical implication is that a prolonged APTT due to FXII deficiency should not trigger empirical anticoagulation or haemostatic prophylaxis, and clinical management should be guided by the overall thrombotic or haemorrhagic risk profile.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings this week have particular relevance to haematology practice in Taiwan. The FCM versus FDI bone safety data from Wagner et al. are immediately practice-relevant: FCM (Ferinject®) is commonly used in Taiwan across gastroenterology, nephrology, cardiology, and haematology services for rapid IV iron repletion[^1]. Haematologists and clinical pharmacists should consider advocating for FDI (Monofer®) as the preferred formulation for patients requiring multiple infusions, particularly women with osteopenia risk, patients with inflammatory bowel disease on long-term iron therapy, and patients receiving dialysis. The NHI formulary listing and relative reimbursement status of FDI in Taiwan should be verified at institutional pharmacy level, as this may affect prescribing flexibility.

The HIT diagnostic data from Zhou et al. are immediately relevant to transfusion medicine and haematology services at NCKUH, NTUH, and VGH[^2]. The identification of ELISA-negative, PEA-positive pathogenic HIT antibodies accounting for up to 65% of platelet-activating IgG in confirmed HIT patients implies that a negative PF4/heparin ELISA does not exclude HIT in a clinically compelling presentation. Centres with access to functional platelet activation assays (PEA or serotonin release assay) should be consulted in such cases; centres without functional assay capacity should be aware that empirical anticoagulation may be warranted in high clinical-probability HIT despite negative ELISA.

The G6PD and α-thalassemia donor RBC survival data from Yee et al. are directly relevant to Taiwan's blood supply[^10]. With α-thalassemia carrier rates of approximately 4–5% and G6PD deficiency rates of approximately 3% in Taiwanese males, a meaningful proportion of donated RBC units may have reduced in vivo survival when transfused to patients with SCD or other haemoglobinopathies. Taiwanese Blood Services could consider whether routine G6PD and α-thalassemia genotyping of repeat donors for haemoglobinopathy patients is feasible and cost-effective, particularly for chronic transfusion programmes.

The AIHA-CLL data from Yan et al. are relevant to haematologists managing the growing population of older Taiwanese patients with CLL, where AIHA occurs in approximately 10% of cases and where the adverse prognostic impact of AIHA can be mitigated by early introduction of BTK inhibitor or venetoclax-based targeted therapy[^11]. The finding that IGHV4-34 usage and DNMT3A mutations are enriched in CLL-associated wAIHA may inform molecular profiling priorities.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

Across this week's literature, four overarching messages emerge for clinical practice. First, the choice of IV iron formulation materially affects skeletal outcomes: FCM causes measurably higher fracture and osteomalacia risk than FDI through a bone-iron-mediated osteoblast suppression mechanism, supporting a transition to FDI in patients requiring multiple infusions. Second, the paradigm for HIT diagnosis is being fundamentally challenged: the finding that a majority of platelet-activating IgG in confirmed HIT patients may be invisible to standard ELISA implies that a negative ELISA cannot exclude HIT in clinically compelling cases, and functional assay access becomes more critical. Third, PF4 emerges as a master immunothrombotic scaffold not only in HIT and VITT but now also in APS, potentially unifying the pathogenesis of these disorders and suggesting shared non-anticoagulant therapeutic targets. Fourth, haemophilia A gene therapy monitoring standards are being refined: OSA is confirmed as the appropriate functional assay for AAV-FVIII-SQ recipients, and biopsy-proven hepatitis should guide — rather than merely trigger — personalised immunosuppression in this setting.

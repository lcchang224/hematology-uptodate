## Summary

The five most clinically actionable signals this week are as follows. First, a comprehensive *Blood* perspective by Fattizzo, Miyakawa, and Broome frames the evolving positioning of sutimlimab (C1s inhibitor) versus B-cell–targeted therapy in cold agglutinin disease (CAD), defining residual unmet needs — including identifying patients who can safely discontinue sutimlimab, improving infection prevention, and managing cold-induced vasomotor symptoms — that have direct implications for therapeutic sequencing in Taiwan where both rituximab-based combinations and sutimlimab are available[^1]. Second, a *Blood* review by Bussel, Hirshman, and Kapur reframes the diagnosis, pathophysiology, and management of anti–HPA-1a fetal-neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia (FNAIT), establishing that Fc fucosylation status — rather than antibody titre or neonatal platelet count alone — more accurately predicts intracranial haemorrhage risk in affected pregnancies, and introducing neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) blockade as an emerging antenatal strategy[^2]. Third, a *Blood Advances* phase 3 report of the explorer8 study by Young et al. provides 56-week efficacy and safety data for concizumab in haemophilia A and B without inhibitors, confirming durable low median annualized bleeding rates (HA: 1.7 [IQR 0.0–4.5]; HB: 2.8 [IQR 0.0–6.4]) and a stable pharmacokinetic profile without new safety signals — representing the longest prospective efficacy data available for this anti-TFPI prophylactic agent[^3]. Fourth, a *Blood Advances* prospective registry analysis of the ADVANCE Japan cohort by Nagao et al. (n = 599 adults with haemophilia aged ≥40 years, 5-year follow-up) demonstrates that 28 first arterial events occurred over 5 years (4.67%), that observed incidence closely matched predictions from Japanese population-based models (Hisayama and Suita), whereas QRISK3 substantially overestimated risk, and that haemophilia-specific clinical factors did not improve predictive accuracy beyond standard scores — informing how arterial thrombosis prevention should be approached in the growing cohort of ageing Taiwanese men with haemophilia[^4]. Fifth, a *Blood* translational study by Sighinolfi et al. using thalassaemic mice and two independent iron-overload models establishes that intracellular iron overload impairs mitochondrial fitness in haematopoietic stem cells, induces metabolic rewiring toward glycolysis, and that in-vivo reduction of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) rescues haematopoietic stem cell quiescence and self-renewal — providing a mechanistic basis for the haematopoietic toxicity of transfusional iron burden in thalassaemia and sickle cell disease[^5].

## I. Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

A brief but clinically significant finding relevant to neonatal thrombocytopenia comes this week not from primary ITP literature, but from a *Blood* comprehensive review by Bussel, Hirshman, and Kapur on anti–HPA-1a FNAIT[^2]. Although FNAIT is pathophysiologically distinct from immune ITP, the review is relevant to haematologists who encounter neonatal thrombocytopenia and platelet alloimmunisation in clinical practice. The review establishes that intracranial haemorrhage occurs in 10–20% of affected fetuses or newborns and that conventional reliance on anti–HPA-1a antibody titres and neonatal platelet counts for severity prediction is insufficient.

The authors synthesise emerging evidence that structural antibody characteristics — specifically decreased Fc fucosylation, which enhances Fcγ receptor-mediated effector functions — more accurately predict disease severity, and that increased Fc galactosylation may further augment complement activation. Beyond platelet-mediated injury, anti–HPA-1a targets megakaryocytes, trophoblasts, and endothelial cells, contributing to placental inflammation and associations with fetal growth restriction and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment including autism. The review introduces FcRn blockade as a novel antenatal strategy operating by two complementary mechanisms: reducing circulating maternal anti–HPA-1a titres and simultaneously inhibiting transplacental IgG transfer. Whether antenatal treatment reduces placental inflammation and neurodevelopmental sequelae beyond thrombocytopenia correction remains under investigation. From a practice standpoint, the review supports universal pregnancy screening for HPA-1bb genotype and DRB3\*01:01 status as the highest-risk combination, enabling risk-stratified antenatal management protocols.

Also notable this week, a *Haematologica* letter by Plümer et al. describes a single case of anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy for multi-refractory immune thrombocytopenia, in which durable remission was achieved alongside a transient expansion of PNH-type cells — a phenomenon previously described after other lymphodepleting immunotherapies[^6]. This observation raises important mechanistic questions about complement dysregulation during CAR T-cell–induced bone marrow reconstitution in non-malignant settings, and is relevant as CAR T-cell therapy begins to be explored in refractory autoimmune cytopenias.

## III. Aplastic Anemia

A *Haematologica* editorial by On and Ram, published 18 June 2026, addresses the ongoing question of whether anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG)-based immunosuppressive therapy (IST) remains the optimal bridging strategy for bone marrow transplant in aplastic anemia, or whether earlier allogeneic transplantation strategies should be pursued more widely[^7]. While the abstract was not available at time of writing, the editorial title — "Bone marrow transplant in aplastic anemia: the horse served us well, but is it still the best ride?" — signals a conceptual reappraisal of the classical IST-first, transplant-second paradigm in the context of improved haploidentical transplant outcomes and the availability of eltrombopag-containing IST regimens.

This editorial contextualises the EHA News item noting an ongoing clinical report on eltrombopag plus cyclosporine A for moderate aplastic anaemia presented by Höchsmann at EHA, reinforcing an international trend toward eltrombopag-containing front-line IST protocols across disease severity strata. Haematologists at NCKUH should note that eltrombopag combined with standard IST (horse-ATG plus ciclosporin) has NHI coverage in Taiwan for severe aplastic anaemia following the 2023 reimbursement expansion.

## IV. PNH — Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria

The *Haematologica* case report by Plümer et al. described in Section I is additionally relevant here, as it documents transient expansion of PNH-type cells following anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy in a patient treated for multi-refractory ITP[^6]. The clinical course was reported as self-limiting without requirement for complement inhibitor therapy, but the case establishes that PNH-type clonal expansion is a potential monitoring consideration following lymphodepleting therapies in non-malignant settings. Haematologists using deep lymphodepletion for autoimmune haematological conditions should include PNH flow cytometry in post-treatment monitoring panels.

Regarding established PNH therapies, the EHA News feed this week archived two previously relevant abstract presentations: an indirect treatment comparison of iptacopan versus pegcetacoplan (Kulasekararaj) and a 2-year safety and efficacy update of iptacopan monotherapy (Risitano) — both from EHA 2024–2025 rather than the current coverage window, and thus not counted as new signals. No new iptacopan or factor D/B inhibitor Phase III data were published in indexed journals this week.

## V. Hemophilia A & B (including gene therapy)

Two papers this week provide clinically actionable updates on non-factor prophylaxis and cardiovascular risk management in haemophilia.

The *Blood Advances* paper by Young et al. presents the 56-week cut-off results of the phase 3 explorer8 study of concizumab in haemophilia A (HA) and B (HB) without inhibitors (NCT04082429)[^3]. Concizumab is a subcutaneous anti-TFPI (tissue factor pathway inhibitor) monoclonal antibody designed for once-daily prophylaxis. Of 148 patients in the full analysis set, 21 were randomised to no prophylaxis, 42 to concizumab, and 85 assigned to non-randomised concizumab groups. Among patients with haemophilia A receiving concizumab, the median annualized bleeding rate for treated spontaneous and traumatic episodes at the 56-week cut-off was 1.7 (IQR 0.0–4.5), and for haemophilia B it was 2.8 (IQR 0.0–6.4). These results are consistent with the earlier 32-week cut-off results, confirming durable effect without evidence of tachyphylaxis. Concizumab plasma concentrations remained stable over time, and no new safety concerns — including no thrombotic events — were reported. This 56-week dataset represents the longest prospective controlled efficacy data available for concizumab and is directly relevant for haematologists evaluating whether to add concizumab to the prophylactic armamentarium alongside emicizumab (for HA) and fitusiran (for HA/B), especially in patients with HB — a group for whom emicizumab is not effective.

The *Blood Advances* study by Nagao et al. from the ADVANCE Japan nationwide prospective registry addresses arterial thrombotic risk in ageing adults with haemophilia[^4]. Among 599 participants with a median age of 59 years (83% HA, 63% severe disease) followed for 5 years, 28 first arterial events occurred — an incidence of 4.67% over the observation period, translating to 2.93 events per 100 participants over 5 years. This observed incidence was closely matched by the Hisayama model (sensitivity 70.5%, specificity 68.0%) and the Suita score (sensitivity 73.3%, specificity 61.2%), while QRISK3 substantially overestimated arterial risk. Importantly, adding haemophilia-specific clinical variables (severity, FVIII/FIX level, inhibitor history, treatment modality) did not meaningfully improve predictive performance beyond established population-based cardiovascular risk scores. This finding supports the use of general-population Japanese cardiovascular risk tools for primary prevention decision-making in Asian adults with haemophilia — a practical and immediately applicable conclusion for Taiwan, where the haemophilia population is similarly ageing and where primary arterial prevention guidance has been uncertain.

A *Haematologica* translational paper by Nardini et al. explores sialylated immunodominant peptides as a strategy to modulate anti-factor VIII T-cell responses in haemophilia A[^8]. Although abstract details were not available at time of writing, this mechanistic work is relevant to the field of inhibitor immunology, as it investigates whether peptide-based tolerisation approaches can leverage glycan modifications to shift T-cell responses toward tolerance rather than immunity — a potential non-gene-therapy path toward inhibitor eradication.

## VII. Thalassemia (alpha & beta; Taiwan-relevant)

Two papers this week address distinct dimensions of thalassaemia biology and transfusion management.

The *Blood* study by Sighinolfi et al. provides a mechanistic investigation of how iron overload (IO) impairs haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) function in thalassaemia[^5]. Using thalassaemic mice (Hbbth3/+) as a model of chronic IO, the authors demonstrated that IO HSCs accumulate elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), exhibit low mitochondrial membrane potential, and show markedly reduced oxidative phosphorylation. These mitochondrial defects were reproduced in two independent IO models — sickle cell disease mice and wild-type mice receiving exogenous iron loading — confirming the iron-specific, rather than disease-specific, nature of the mechanism. IO HSCs were found to be highly proliferating and, due to mitochondrial dysfunction, dependent on glycolysis for energy production rather than normal oxidative phosphorylation. Crucially, in-vivo iron reduction rescued mitochondrial function, and targeted in-vivo suppression of mtROS improved HSC quiescence and self-renewal. Complementing this, a companion editorial by Stolla published in the same issue of *Blood* contextualises these findings within the broader question of when iron becomes deleterious rather than beneficial for HSC biology[^9]. These results have direct clinical implications for Taiwanese patients with transfusion-dependent thalassaemia major: they provide a cellular mechanistic rationale for aggressive iron chelation not merely to protect end-organ function (cardiac and hepatic), but to preserve the haematopoietic stem cell pool — a consideration particularly relevant when curative stem cell transplantation or gene therapy is being planned.

The *Blood* study by Coyne et al. published 24 June 2026 advances understanding of the molecular control of fetal haemoglobin expression[^10]. The authors demonstrate that IGF2BP1, an m6A RNA-binding protein that is heterochronically silenced in adult erythropoiesis, regulates HBG1/2 (γ-globin) expression through two distinct mechanisms: indirectly by binding and activating HIC2 (itself a BCL11A repressor) and directly by binding to HBG2 transcripts at stop codon–proximal m6A-modified coding sequences to promote γ-globin translation. This dual regulatory mechanism suggests that IGF2BP1 reactivation could achieve fetal haemoglobin induction via pathways independent of and complementary to BCL11A knockdown — the mechanistic basis of existing CRISPR-based therapies. While currently a translational finding, this work has long-term relevance for the development of next-generation HbF-inducing strategies applicable to both β-thalassaemia and sickle cell disease.

Regarding red blood cell transfusion in sickle cell disease, the *Blood Advances* observational trial by Yee et al. (NCT04426591, n = 22 biotin-labelled transfusion episodes from 20 recipients) quantified donor RBC survival in patients on chronic transfusion therapy[^11]. Median posttransfusion recovery was 97.3% at 24 hours, 80% at 28 days, and 21% at 90 days, with a median time to 50% RBC recovery of 60 days (range 23–85 days). In multivariate mixed-effects analysis, recipient spleen volume and donor G6PD deficiency or α-thalassaemia trait were significantly associated with decreased donor RBC survival. These findings are directly applicable to Taiwan's transfusion medicine context: G6PD deficiency affects approximately 3–5% of Taiwanese males, and α-thalassaemia trait is present in approximately 4–5% of the population, meaning a meaningful proportion of blood bank donors may carry variants that reduce transfusion efficacy in chronically transfused haematological patients.

## VIII. Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA)

A clinically important *Blood* perspective article by Fattizzo, Miyakawa, and Broome published 25 June 2026 synthesises the current evidence base and articulates the optimal positioning of sutimlimab versus B-cell–targeted therapy in cold agglutinin disease (CAD)[^1]. The perspective recapitulates CAD pathophysiology: cold agglutinins are produced by a clonal B-lymphocyte population (recognised by WHO as a low-grade lymphoproliferative disorder) that triggers haemolysis via classical complement pathway activation. B-cell–targeted therapy with rituximab alone yields partial responses in approximately 50% of patients, whereas the combination of rituximab with bendamustine substantially increases and prolongs response rates, albeit at the cost of significant infection risk. Sutimlimab, the first approved C1s complement inhibitor, rapidly and sustainably increases haemoglobin levels, reduces haemolysis markers, and significantly improves quality of life; however, it does not deplete the pathogenic B-cell clone or reduce cold agglutinin titres.

The authors identify several clinically important unmet needs: the inability to predict which patients can safely discontinue sutimlimab while maintaining remission, the incomplete control of cold-induced vasomotor symptoms (acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon) that persist despite complement inhibition because these symptoms are cold agglutinin-mediated rather than complement-mediated, and the optimal infection prevention strategy given the risk of encapsulated organism infection with C1s inhibition. The perspective also addresses combination strategies that might provide simultaneous B-cell clone control and rapid complement blockade, although no Phase III data currently support combination use. For haematologists at NCKUH, this article represents the most current expert framework for sequencing sutimlimab and rituximab-based regimens in CAD, a disease where treatment decisions are frequently made without access to randomised head-to-head trial data.

## IX. Red Cell Disorders (G6PD, PK deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis)

The *Blood Advances* observational trial by Yee et al. on RBC transfusion survival in sickle cell disease (described in Section VII) is also relevant here for its documentation that donor G6PD deficiency and α-thalassaemia trait are significantly associated with reduced transfusion RBC survival in multivariate analysis[^11]. This finding has implications beyond sickle cell disease and applies to any clinical situation where RBC longevity after transfusion is critical, including patients with severe hereditary haemolytic anaemias receiving chronic support transfusions. The absence of routine G6PD and haemoglobinopathy screening for blood bank donors in most Taiwanese institutions means that this source of transfusion inefficiency is currently uncharacterised in the local context.

A structured variant interpretation framework for idiopathic erythrocytosis published in *BJH* by Giannella et al. uses targeted NGS combined with ACMG criteria, population data, computational predictions, and structural modelling to prioritise rare variants in a clinical cohort[^12]. This practical framework is relevant for haematologists encountering JAK2-negative polycythaemia in clinical practice, where the differential includes primary familial erythrocytosis (EPOR, VHL, PHD2/EGLN1), high-affinity haemoglobin variants, and other rare aetiologies. The study underscores the genetic heterogeneity of this condition and the value of integrating multiple lines of evidence — rather than single-gene testing — in variant classification.

## X. Iron Deficiency & Nutritional Anemias

The *BJH* study by Wu et al. investigates the role of WTAP (Wilms tumour 1-associating protein), a core component of the m6A methyltransferase complex, in regulating erythropoiesis[^13]. The study demonstrates that WTAP is highly expressed in erythroid progenitors and that its depletion compromises erythropoiesis both in vivo and in vitro, with a reduction in erythroid progenitor numbers and increased erythroblast apoptosis. Mechanistically, WTAP-mediated m6A modification sustains erythropoiesis by maintaining STAT5 mRNA stability and downstream signalling, though the authors acknowledge that STAT5 is a critical but not exclusive effector. While currently a mechanistic rather than clinical study, this work contributes to an emerging framework understanding epitranscriptomic regulation of erythropoiesis — relevant to understanding anaemia of chronic inflammation and ineffective erythropoiesis conditions prevalent in NCKUH's patient population.

## XI. Thrombosis & Coagulation (VTE, APS, inherited thrombophilia)

A *Blood Advances* systematic review and meta-analysis by Kawtharany et al. addresses secondary anticoagulation prophylaxis for children with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)[^14]. Although the full abstract was not available at time of writing, this paper directly extends the framework established by last week's W25 ASH/ISTH paediatric VTE guidelines (Betensky et al.) by focusing specifically on the secondary prevention context in paediatric APS — a clinically distinct and challenging population where the optimal anticoagulant type (vitamin K antagonist, LMWH, or DOAC), target INR, and duration of treatment are all debated. This meta-analysis is expected to inform clinical decisions at institutions managing paediatric APS, and its conclusions will complement the GRADE-based framework already reported in W25.

Regarding thrombosis biology, a *Blood* study by Zang et al. — published 18 June 2026 and just outside the strict W25 coverage window — identifies neutrophil-derived serpin B3 as a driver of thrombus stiffening in acute ischaemic stroke (AIS)[^15]. In a proteomic analysis of stroke thrombi from 34 patients stratified by 90-day outcomes, serpin B3 emerged as the neutrophil-enriched protein most strongly correlated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, secreted serpin B3 promotes thrombus collagen deposition and stiffening through upregulation of TGF-β1, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and COL1A1. In murine models, Serpinb3a knockdown delayed vascular occlusion, improved thrombolysis efficiency, and resulted in better neurological recovery. While this is primarily a neurology-thrombosis interface study, it establishes SERPINB3 as both a biomarker and potential therapeutic target for thrombolysis resistance — a topic relevant to haematologists who participate in multidisciplinary stroke management teams.

## XIII. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several of this week's findings carry specific relevance for haematological practice in Taiwan. The sutimlimab/CAD positioning article[^1] is pertinent because CAD, although rare, is increasingly recognised as distinct from warm AIHA and requires a treatment algorithm that balances the rapid haemoglobin response achievable with sutimlimab against the long-term clone-directed efficacy of rituximab–bendamustine. Taiwan's NHI currently covers rituximab for AIHA, but sutimlimab reimbursement for CAD requires ongoing pharmacoeconomic review; the Fattizzo perspective provides the evidence framework for this evaluation.

The concizumab explorer8 56-week data[^3] are relevant because haemophilia B patients in Taiwan — approximately 20–25% of the registered haemophilia population — lack access to an approved non-factor prophylactic equivalent to emicizumab. Concizumab covers both HA and HB regardless of inhibitor status, and these 56-week data meaningfully strengthen the evidence base for potential NHI submission. The ADVANCE Japan arterial risk data[^4] are immediately applicable: the haemophilia population at CGMH, NTUH, and NCKUH is ageing, and Japanese population-based cardiovascular models (Hisayama, Suita) that performed well in the Japanese cohort are likely to have greater generalisability to Taiwanese patients than QRISK3, which was developed on a Northern European dataset.

Taiwan's high prevalence of α-thalassaemia trait (~4–5%) and G6PD deficiency (~3–5% in males) is directly implicated in the Yee et al. RBC survival data[^11]: donor screening for these variants is not current transfusion medicine practice in Taiwan, yet the published data suggest this may reduce efficacy of chronic transfusion programmes in thalassaemia major and sickle cell disease (the latter now seen in Taiwan's immigrant and ethnically diverse populations). The Sighinolfi et al. iron overload/HSC mitochondria study[^5] supports aggressive chelation to preserve HSC function in transfusion-dependent thalassaemia patients being considered for curative stem cell transplantation or gene therapy — programmes that are operational at NTUH and CGMH.

## XIV. Key Takeaways

The most clinically actionable messages from this week's literature are as follows. In cold agglutinin disease, sutimlimab and B-cell–directed therapy address different disease mechanisms (complement inhibition vs. clone depletion) and neither alone resolves all disease manifestations; the optimal therapeutic sequence and combination remains an unmet need requiring prospective evaluation. In FNAIT, Fc fucosylation status should replace antibody titre and neonatal platelet count as the primary severity predictor, and FcRn blockade is emerging as a novel antenatal strategy that simultaneously lowers maternal antibody and prevents transplacental transfer. In haemophilia, concizumab maintains durable efficacy at 56 weeks in both HA and HB without inhibitors, while arterial cardiovascular risk in ageing Asian men with haemophilia is real and well-predicted by Japanese population-based models. In thalassaemia, iron overload damages HSC mitochondria and rewires metabolism toward glycolysis in a reversible manner, providing a mechanistic rationale for early and aggressive chelation when transplant or gene therapy is planned. Finally, donor G6PD deficiency and α-thalassaemia trait — prevalent in Taiwan's donor population — meaningfully reduce transfusion RBC survival and warrant consideration in chronic transfusion programme design.

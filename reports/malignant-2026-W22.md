## Summary

Five signals dominate this week's literature. **(1)** A phase 1 trial of anti-CD117 antibody briquilimab combined with nonmyeloablative conditioning in 32 older adults with AML/MDS demonstrates feasibility with zero primary graft failures; 1-year EFS was 69.2% and OS 75% in AML-CR patients, establishing a novel antibody-based conditioning platform.[^1] **(2)** A cost-efficient smMIP-based NGS MRD assay applied to 93 AML patients pre-allogeneic HCT identifies MRD positivity as an independent predictor of inferior OS (HR 4.58, 95% CI not fully reported, p = 0.002), at a library preparation cost of approximately €8 per sample.[^2] **(3)** Single-cell multiomics of DLBCL paired diagnostic and relapsed/refractory samples identifies CSNK1E as a critical mediator of drug persistence in stemlike memory B-cell–like cells; CSNK1E inhibition potentiates R-CHOP efficacy both in vitro and in vivo.[^3] **(4)** A German real-world registry of 606 RRMM patients confirms that standard-of-care cilta-cel in earlier lines (1–3 prior therapies) achieves 12-month PFS of 79% versus 70% in later lines, with non-ICANS neurotoxicity occurring less frequently in the early group (3% vs 8%).[^4] **(5)** A comprehensive spatiotemporal single-cell atlas of the bone marrow microenvironment during CML progression, using a 54-marker CODEX panel profiling over 2 million cells, reveals progressive vascular disorganization, PD-1 upregulation on CD8+ T cells, and megakaryocyte morphological abnormalities that are partially reversed by TKI therapy.[^5]

## I. AML — Acute Myeloid Leukemia

Three distinct papers advance the AML field this week across conditioning platforms, MRD methodology, and disease biology.

**CD117-targeted conditioning with briquilimab.** Published in *Blood*, Muffly and colleagues report the first clinical results of briquilimab — a monoclonal antibody blocking stem cell factor binding to CD117 (c-Kit) — as an addition to nonmyeloablative fludarabine/TBI conditioning in older adults with AML or MDS undergoing matched-donor allogeneic HCT (NCT04429191).[^1] Thirty-two patients were enrolled (13 AML in CR, 3 AML in relapse, 16 MDS); median age was 70 years and most had detectable MRD at screening. Briquilimab was administered 10–14 days before day 0, producing a mean 62.4% ± 22.7% depletion of AML/MDS HSPCs in marrow and a threefold increase in serum SCF, confirming pharmacodynamic target engagement. There were no infusion reactions, dose-limiting toxicities, or primary graft failure events. Among the AML-in-CR cohort, 1-year EFS was 69.2% and 1-year OS was 75%; among the MDS cohort, 1-year EFS was 53.8% and 1-year OS was 76.4%. The single relapsed-AML patient who responded experienced only a transient remission. These proof-of-concept data suggest that CD117-antibody–based myelosuppression could eventually replace or augment cytotoxic conditioning, sparing toxicity in older and frailer patients. A companion editorial from Manz contextualizes how CD117-targeted HCT preconditioning may synergize with emerging graft-facilitating agents in AML/MDS.[^6]

**Cost-efficient NGS MRD assay predicts post-HCT outcomes.** A *Haematologica* study by Krauß and colleagues validates a smMIP (single-molecule molecular inversion probe)–based NGS panel targeting 92 loci across 33 driver genes in 93 AML patients in remission prior to allo-SCT.[^2] MRD positivity — defined as ≥1 non-DTA variant with VAF ≥0.5% — independently predicted inferior OS in multivariable analysis (HR 4.58; p = 0.002). The 1-year cumulative incidence of relapse was 35% in MRD-positive versus 25% in MRD-negative patients (p < 0.001). Importantly, library preparation costs were approximately €8 per sample, making this assay potentially deployable in resource-limited settings. Intriguingly, three patients harbored multiple low-VAF clustered RUNX1 and TET2 variants pre-transplantation, potentially reflecting treatment-induced mutagenesis — a signal warranting prospective investigation. A complementary CIBMTR analysis in the same issue, examining 2,544 AML-CR1 patients undergoing alloHCT between 2013 and 2019, confirms that MFC-MRD positivity (present in 11%) predicts inferior OS (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06–1.51; p = 0.009), inferior DFS (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.11–1.53; p = 0.001), and increased relapse (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.17–1.72; p < 0.001), but finds substantial inter-center variability that underscores the urgent need for methodological standardization.[^7]

**Mutant IDH1 epigenetically blocks neutropoiesis.** A mechanistic study in *Blood* by Hakobyan and colleagues dissects why IDH1 mutations are particularly associated with myeloid neoplasms.[^8] Using DNA methylation mapping and a genetically engineered inducible Idh1-mutant murine model, the group demonstrates that IDH1 (but not IDH2) mutation causes cell-intrinsic neutrophil differentiation block through epigenetic repression of the C/EBPε transcription factor encoded by *CEBPE*, a key regulator of terminal granulopoiesis. IDH1 expression is strongly upregulated physiologically in myeloid progenitors in both human and murine hematopoiesis, explaining the tissue-specific vulnerability. Critically, the block is reversible: overexpression of C/EBPα (upstream of C/EBPε) or treatment with hypomethylating agents restored differentiation in vitro. This reversibility has direct therapeutic implications — it provides mechanistic rationale for combining IDH1 inhibitors with HMAs in IDH1-mutant AML and suggests that differentiation therapy windows may be exploitable even without targeted IDH1 inhibition.

**Menin inhibitor–induced pseudoprogression.** A brief technical report in *Blood* from Helman and Stonestrom describes the morphologic and flow cytometric features of pseudoprogression observed in KMT2A-rearranged AML patients treated with menin inhibitors.[^9] As menin inhibitors advance toward regulatory approval, recognizing this phenomenon — bone marrow blast increase without true disease progression, driven by differentiation rather than expansion — becomes clinically critical to avoid premature treatment discontinuation.

## II. ALL — Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

Two biologically distinct papers reshape understanding of ALL pathogenesis and therapeutic targeting this week.

**TCF3::HLF B-ALL and the IL-1β self-reinforcing axis.** Published in *Blood*, Suzuki and colleagues establish a novel mouse model faithfully recapitulating human TCF3::HLF B-ALL — among the most aggressive and treatment-refractory ALL subtypes — including osteolytic bone lesions.[^10] Single-cell RNA sequencing of patient samples revealed markedly upregulated *IL1B* at relapse compared with diagnosis. Mechanistically, TCF3::HLF directly binds an intronic regulatory element in the *IL1B* locus, driving transcription. Genetic deletion of *IL1B* or its receptor *IL1R1* suppressed leukemic growth and reduced RANKL expression, thereby ameliorating bone destruction. This IL-1β–RANKL axis represents a therapeutically actionable target in a disease for which no effective therapy currently exists, and anakinra or other IL-1 blockade strategies are now biologically motivated for clinical investigation.

**IGF2BP2 as an oncogenic driver in peripheral T-cell lymphoma.** A study in *Blood* by Hu and colleagues identifies the m6A reader IGF2BP2 as consistently overexpressed across common nodal PTCL subtypes in four independent cohorts including an RNA-seq dataset of 196 newly diagnosed PTCL patients.[^11] IGF2BP2 promoted tumor proliferation and suppressed CD8+ T-cell infiltration by stabilizing endosome-related transcripts (RAB4, VPS35, RAB9, STAM), enhancing endocytic activity and membrane protein internalization. Therapeutic targeting with the small-molecule CWI1-2 suppressed endocytosis and impeded tumor growth in both cell lines and patient-derived xenograft models. While this work is primarily translational, it identifies RNA m6A modification as a targetable vulnerability in PTCL — a disease where standard CHOP-based chemotherapy achieves only modest results. Although PTCL is discussed here in the context of ALL, it is addressed more fully in Section IX.

## III. CML — Chronic Myeloid Leukemia

Two landmark publications mark the 25th anniversary of imatinib approval in CML this week, providing both narrative synthesis and spatiotemporal biological data.

**Twenty-five years of TKI therapy: from fatal disease to functional cure.** A comprehensive anniversary review in *Leukemia* by Hochhaus, Talpaz, Saglio, Cortes and colleagues traces the transformation of CML management since imatinib's approval in 2001.[^12] The authors review how approximately 50% of eligible patients in landmark TFR trials achieve and maintain treatment-free remission, and discuss emerging strategies including fourth-generation TKIs, BCR::ABL1 PROTAC degraders, and immunomodulatory combinations that may expand TFR eligibility further. The review critically appraises remaining challenges: TKI resistance mechanisms (particularly T315I gating mutations), long-term cardiovascular toxicity with ponatinib and nilotinib, affordability gaps in resource-limited settings, and the relatively narrow proportion of patients who qualify for TFR attempts under current criteria. A companion editorial by Gale and Hochhaus contextualizes the 25-year journey for the journal readership.[^13]

**Spatiotemporal single-cell atlas of the CML bone marrow microenvironment.** Li and colleagues publish a technically ambitious *Blood* paper profiling 2,033,725 cells from 55 regions of interest in CML and wild-type mouse femurs using a 54-marker CODEX panel, complemented by 3D microscopy.[^5] During CML progression, the group observed myeloid and progenitor expansion, increased PD-L1+ leukemic cells, PD-1 upregulation on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells indicative of localized immune exhaustion, and near-complete loss of B cells, plasma cells, and bone cells. Advanced CML showed striking expansion of immature, pericyte-deficient vasculature that disrupted hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell niches. Megakaryocytes in CML mice and patient biopsies were morphologically irregular, with reduced cytoskeletal gene expression at diagnosis that was reversed following TKI therapy. In direct contrast, AML mice had reduced vasculature and fewer MKs but the remaining MKs retained normal morphology — defining a CML-specific microenvironmental signature. This atlas provides the most granular spatial map of leukemogenesis in a myeloproliferative context to date and identifies PD-1 pathway and vascular remodeling as potential combination targets alongside TKI therapy.

## IV. CLL / SLL — Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia

**AI-enhanced flow cytometry accelerates CLL MRD detection.** Published in *Leukemia*, Chiu and colleagues report CCADDAS — a cloud-deployed, largely unsupervised AI pipeline for CLL MRD detection using a single-tube 10-color flow cytometry panel.[^14] Applied to 166 MRD-positive and 61 MRD-negative cases, the algorithm performs automated error correction, clustering (PARC), dimensionality reduction (UMAP), anomaly detection against normal controls, and cluster-informed downsampling that preserves rare MRD events. Concordance with expert manual review was excellent (R² = 0.98), and an AI-generated aberrancy scale distinguished CLL MRD from background B cells with an AUC of 0.98. Downsampling reduced cellularity by 85% and file size by 78%. Manual analysis time fell from 9.0 to 0.9 minutes per case — a 90% reduction — with no compromise in test performance. As flow cytometric MRD testing becomes a regulatory endpoint in multiple CLL trials including fixed-duration ibrutinib/venetoclax combinations, this pipeline could substantially reduce the technical barrier to MRD-guided care, particularly at lower-volume centers.

## VII. DLBCL & Aggressive B-cell Lymphoma

**CSNK1E sustains stemlike drug persistence in DLBCL.** Published in *Blood* and accompanied by an editorial from Knittel and Reinhardt, Li and colleagues perform single-cell RNA and BCR sequencing on paired diagnostic and R/R samples from 8 DLBCL patients, validated in 3 independent cohorts.[^3] Drug-persistent cells — those surviving first-line R-CHOP — displayed a transcriptional profile characteristic of a less-differentiated, memory B-cell–like state with enhanced stemlike properties that independently correlated with unfavorable clinical outcomes across multiple cohorts. Functionally, drug-persistent cells exhibited significantly increased clonogenicity in vitro and tumor-initiating capacity in vivo. The WNT pathway kinase CSNK1E (casein kinase 1ε) was specifically upregulated in these stemlike cells through activation of the APRIL–TNFRSF13B axis. Pharmacological CSNK1E inhibition impaired the growth and tumor-initiating capacity of drug-persistent cells and potentiated R-CHOP efficacy in preclinical models. Given that up to 40% of DLBCL patients experience relapsed/refractory disease after front-line immunochemotherapy, CSNK1E represents a mechanistically grounded therapeutic target; the APRIL–TNFRSF13B–CSNK1E axis now provides a molecular framework for understanding why some patients harbor persistent disease despite apparent initial remission.

**Cardiovascular adverse events with bispecific antibodies in R/R NHL.** A FAERS-based pharmacovigilance analysis in *Journal of Hematology & Oncology* by Munir and colleagues evaluates mosunetuzumab, glofitamab, and epcoritamab.[^15] Among 246,490 total adverse event reports, 1,931 involved bispecific antibodies, and CVAEs occurred in 336 (17.4%) of these, of which 132 (39.3%) were fatal. As a drug class, bispecifics were associated with increased reporting of hypotension (adjusted reporting odds ratio [aROR] 2.15, 95% CI 1.48–3.13) and fatal CVAEs (aROR 2.04, 95% CI 1.60–2.60) versus all other drugs in the database. Drug-specific signals diverged importantly: epcoritamab carried the strongest hypotension signal (aROR 2.37, 95% CI 1.55–3.62) and fatal CVAE reporting (aROR 2.26, 95% CI 1.72–2.98), whereas mosunetuzumab was associated with atrial fibrillation/flutter (aROR 3.04, 95% CI 1.53–6.03), supraventricular tachycardia (aROR 2.72, 95% CI 1.42–5.21), and tachyarrhythmias (aROR 2.60, 95% CI 1.44–4.72). CVAEs occurred earlier than non-CVAEs (median 10 vs 16 days; p < 0.001). Cytokine release syndrome co-occurred in 33.4% of bispecific-related adverse events, with hypotension most commonly overlapping (53.8%), while most arrhythmias arose independently of CRS — suggesting distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. As bispecifics expand into second-line and potentially first-line DLBCL settings, cardiovascular risk stratification and proactive monitoring will require formal incorporation into clinical management algorithms.

## IX. Hodgkin Lymphoma & T-cell Lymphoma

**Nivolumab with or without vinblastine for elderly unfit HL: NIVINIHO trial.** The LYSA phase II NIVINIHO trial (NCT03580408) reports outcomes in 64 treatment-naive HL patients aged ≥61 years (median age 75 years) with comorbidities contraindicating standard chemotherapy.[^16] Ann Arbor stage III–IV disease was present in 75% of patients. Following nivolumab induction, 11 patients (17.2%) achieved complete metabolic response (CMR) and proceeded to nivolumab consolidation alone; 23 patients (35.9%) with stable disease or partial response received nivolumab plus vinblastine. Among 56 evaluable patients, only 16 (28.6%) achieved CMR at end of treatment. With a median follow-up of 24.4 months, median PFS was 9.8 months (95% CI 4.2–12.0), and the 2-year OS rate was 74.1% (95% CI 58.9–84.4%). Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 50% of patients, and immune-related adverse events led to treatment discontinuation in 19 patients (29.7%). These results confirm that anti-PD-1 monotherapy is tolerable in elderly HL but provides only modest disease control; the low CMR rate highlights the continued unmet need in this population, and combination strategies incorporating brentuximab vedotin or low-dose alkylating agents merit prospective evaluation.

**CSNK1E in CTCL and multiomic evolution.** A comprehensive multiomics study of 34 CTCL patients using exome, whole-genome, epigenome, and single-cell RNA/TCR sequencing of 99 clinically annotated serial samples by Dorando and colleagues in *Blood* identifies recurrent progression-associated alterations including CCR4 mutations, PI3K pathway activation, and a gain-of-function STAT3 D661Y mutation that enhances Rho GTPase pathway gene expression.[^17] EZH2 mutations were recurrently progression-associated, nominating EZH2 inhibitors as rational candidates in genomically selected CTCL. These data strongly support routine genomic profiling at CTCL progression to guide biomarker-informed therapeutic decisions.

**IGF2BP2 in PTCL.** As detailed in Section II, the *Blood* study by Hu and colleagues establishes IGF2BP2-driven endocytosis as a targetable oncogenic mechanism in nodal PTCL.[^11] CWI1-2, a small-molecule IGF2BP2 inhibitor, demonstrated anti-tumor activity in patient-derived xenograft models, providing the first proof-of-concept that m6A reader targeting can simultaneously suppress tumor growth and restore CD8+ T-cell–mediated cytotoxicity — an attractive dual mechanism in a tumor type notorious for immune evasion.

## X. NK/T-cell Lymphoma (Asia focus)

**Epigenetic vulnerability in extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma.** An editorial commentary in *Haematologica* by Ahearne highlights emerging preclinical evidence for an "epigenetic Achilles' heel" in ENKTCL, noting the EZH2 dysregulation data described in the CTCL multiomics study and drawing parallels to epigenetic vulnerabilities in NK/T-cell lineage tumors more broadly.[^18] While no original dataset specific to ENKTCL is published this week, this commentary serves as a timely alert that epigenetic targeting — particularly HDAC and EZH2 inhibition — is a rapidly converging focus across both cutaneous and extranodal T/NK-cell malignancies. Given the disproportionate prevalence of ENKTCL in East Asia, including Taiwan, this trajectory warrants close monitoring.

## XI. Multiple Myeloma

**Cilta-cel in earlier versus later lines: German real-world registry.** A *Journal of Hematology & Oncology* analysis by Gagelmann and colleagues examines all standard-of-care cilta-cel recipients in the German Registry for Stem Cell Transplantation and Cellular Therapy between 2022 and 2025 (n = 606).[^4] Patients were stratified as Early (1–3 prior lines, n = 177, 30%) or Late (>3 prior lines, n = 429, 70%). The overall response rate was 91% in the Early and 88% in the Late group; complete response rates were 63% versus 54%, respectively. The 12-month PFS was 79% for Early versus 70% for Late cilta-cel. Depth of response was the strongest predictor of PFS irrespective of treatment line, with patients maintaining CR showing 100% 12-month PFS in both cohorts. Extramedullary disease was adversely prognostic in both groups, whereas high-risk cytogenetics were not associated with inferior PFS specifically in the Early group — a clinically important observation suggesting that early deployment may partially overcome cytogenetic risk. Non-ICANS neurotoxicity occurred less frequently in the Early group (3% vs 8%), while non-relapse mortality was comparable (6% vs 7%). These data provide the most granular real-world validation to date of the CARTITUDE-4 finding and support deploying cilta-cel as early as first relapse in lenalidomide-refractory patients.

**Twice-weekly ixazomib/pomalidomide/dexamethasone in RRMM.** A phase I/II trial in *Haematologica* by Nadeem and colleagues evaluates a fully oral triplet regimen in 50 RRMM patients (median 2 prior lines; 98% prior lenalidomide, 88% prior bortezomib).[^19] The recommended phase II dose was ixazomib 4 mg on days 1, 4, 8, and 11 plus pomalidomide 4 mg (days 1–14) and dexamethasone 8 mg in 21-day cycles. In all 50 patients, the ORR was 60.0% (≥VGPR in 24%), with median duration of response 18.0 months and median PFS 13.9 months; the 3-year OS rate was 85.2%. In the 38 patients receiving the RP2D, ORR was 65.8% (≥VGPR 28.9%), median DOR 19.3 months, median PFS 17.8 months, and 3-year OS 80.3%. Common grade ≥3 toxicities were neutropenia (22%) and leukopenia (22%). Two dose-limiting toxicities were observed during escalation. While the ORR and PFS are modest compared with contemporary triplets incorporating daratumumab, the all-oral administration schedule offers practical advantages for patients who cannot tolerate frequent clinic visits — a consideration particularly relevant in elderly or rural Taiwanese patients.

## XII. Transplant & Cellular Therapy (CAR-T / HSCT / GVHD)

**HLH-like toxicities after immune effector cell therapy: management framework.** Johnson, McNerney, Frank, and Shah publish a "How I treat" review in *Blood* distinguishing two recognized CAR-associated hyperinflammatory syndromes: IEC-HS (immune effector cell–associated HLH-like syndrome), which emerges after CRS subsides, and CRS-MOD (CRS with multi-organ dysfunction), which progresses with worsening CRS and does not respond to standard CRS management.[^20] The authors provide a diagnostic framework integrating ferritin kinetics, sCD25 levels, bone marrow hemophagocytosis, and fibrinogen trajectories, and outline a staged therapeutic approach incorporating anakinra, ruxolitinib, etoposide, and — in refractory IEC-HS — alemtuzumab or plasma exchange. As bispecific T-cell engagers and CAR-T cell use expands in Taiwan (cilta-cel and ide-cel for myeloma; axi-cel and tisagenlecleucel for lymphoma), this operationalized framework provides actionable guidance for centers managing these life-threatening complications.

**Reassessment of anti-CAR T-cell expansion strategies.** An editorial in *Blood* by Bollard and Heslop reviews current strategies for enhancing CAR T-cell expansion in vivo, arguing that antigen-boosting approaches that failed in the HIV context (as demonstrated concurrently in a separate nonhuman primate study) may require fundamental redesign for malignant indications as well — particularly in disease contexts with antigen-low or antigen-heterogeneous tumor targets.[^21]

**In vivo CAR gene delivery: clinical proof-of-concept.** A review by Wagner, Elsallab, and Maus in *Blood* summarizes emerging clinical reports demonstrating feasibility of in vivo CAR T-cell generation — delivering CAR genes directly into the patient's T cells using viral or lipid-nanoparticle vectors — without ex vivo manufacturing.[^22] While efficacy data remain preliminary compared with approved ex vivo products, the approach could dramatically reduce manufacturing time and cost, addressing the access gap in resource-limited settings. This is directly relevant to Taiwan, where the manufacturing timeline for autologous CAR-T products remains a practical barrier at most centers.

**Glofitamab after CAR T-cell failure in non-DLBCL B-cell lymphomas.** A *Haematologica* case series by Sesques and colleagues documents prolonged survival with glofitamab in patients with non-DLBCL histologies (including follicular lymphoma transforming post-CAR T and marginal zone lymphoma) who relapsed after CD19 CAR-T therapy.[^23] While underpowered for formal efficacy conclusions, this series suggests that CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies retain activity even following CD19-targeted CAR-T failure — in part because glofitamab targets a distinct antigen — and supports prospective investigation of sequential bispecific strategies post-CAR T relapse.

## XIV. Taiwan Clinical Context

Several findings published this week carry direct relevance for clinical practice at NCKUH and major Taiwanese hematology centers. The German cilta-cel registry data[^4] supporting early-line deployment is particularly timely: Taiwan's NHI expanded cilta-cel reimbursement in late 2025 to include second-line RRMM, and the 79% 12-month PFS in 1–3 prior lines — accompanied by lower non-ICANS neurotoxicity (3%) — provides real-world evidence that should facilitate informed patient counseling. The HLH-like toxicity management framework[^20] is immediately actionable for centers at NTUH, CGMH, VGH-Taipei, and CMUH that are currently operating CAR-T cell therapy programs.

The ENKTCL epigenetic commentary[^18] is of particular regional significance: extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma accounts for approximately 5–10% of all lymphoma diagnoses in Taiwan (vs < 1% in Western populations), and any evidence pointing toward EZH2 or epigenetic vulnerabilities in this lineage deserves prospective evaluation in Taiwan-based cohorts. The ongoing collaboration between NCKUH, CGMH, and the Taiwan Lymphoma Study Group (TLSG) provides an infrastructure for such translational work.

The cost-efficient smMIP-based NGS MRD assay (~€8/sample)[^2] could address a significant access gap in NGS-based MRD monitoring at community hospitals affiliated with the NHI system, where reimbursement for high-complexity molecular assays remains limited. Validation in a Taiwan AML cohort would be a straightforward next step.

Finally, the AI-enhanced CLL MRD pipeline (CCADDAS)[^14] achieving 90% reduction in analysis time is relevant to centers performing high-volume flow cytometric MRD testing under fixed-duration CLL regimens — specifically venetoclax-based doublets and triplets where MRD-guided treatment cessation is increasingly supported by NHI.

## XV. Key Takeaways

Six practice-relevant conclusions emerge from 2026-W22:

First, briquilimab-based CD117-targeting provides a viable nonmyeloablative conditioning platform for older AML/MDS patients with zero primary graft failures and acceptable 1-year survival outcomes — a potential paradigm shift in reduced-intensity transplantation.[^1]

Second, low-cost smMIP-based NGS MRD (HR 4.58, p = 0.002) achieves clinically relevant risk stratification before allo-HCT at ~€8/sample, enabling broad real-world implementation beyond academic centers.[^2]

Third, CSNK1E-driven stemlike drug persistence defines a mechanistically distinct relapse pathway in DLBCL that is independent of conventional resistance mechanisms and pharmacologically targetable in preclinical models.[^3]

Fourth, real-world cilta-cel data from 606 German patients confirm that early deployment (1–3 prior lines) achieves 12-month PFS of 79% with a more favorable neurotoxicity profile than late-line use, supporting front-line-adjacent positioning of BCMA CAR-T therapy.[^4]

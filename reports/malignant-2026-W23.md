## Summary

Five signals dominate this week's literature. **(1)** Five-year survival data from TRANSCEND NHL 001 establish lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) as a potentially curative therapy in R/R LBCL, with 5-year OS of 38% among efficacy-evaluable patients and 5-year OS of 78% in the long-term follow-up cohort who remained alive at end of study, demonstrating that most deaths occur within the first 2 years post-infusion[^1]. **(2)** The KOMET-007 phase 1 trial reports ziftomenib 600 mg combined with venetoclax/azacitidine in R/R NPM1-mutated AML, achieving a composite complete remission rate of 46% overall and 70% in venetoclax-naïve patients, with 67% central MRD negativity — the first clinical data for a menin inhibitor triplet in this molecularly defined setting[^2]. **(3)** A landmark MRD-dynamics analysis from the randomized phase 3 IMROZ study demonstrates that Isa-VRd produces deeper and more sustained MRD negativity than VRd in transplant-ineligible NDMM through 60 months of follow-up, with time to progression significantly prolonged even in patients who converted from MRD-negative to MRD-positive status[^3]. **(4)** A phase 1 study of arlocabtagene autoleucel (arlo-cel), a GPRC5D-directed CAR T-cell therapy, in 84 heavily pretreated RRMM patients reports an ORR of 87%, CR rate of 53%, median PFS of 18.3 months (95% CI 11.8–21.9), and a 1-year OS rate of 90% at a median follow-up of 16.1 months[^4]. **(5)** A mechanistic study identifies CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and functionally active CD4+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells — mediated via IL-21 and CXCL13 signaling — as dual predictors of glofitamab resistance in patient-derived B-NHL spheroids, with anti-TIGIT co-treatment partially overcoming resistance ex vivo[^5].

## I. AML — Acute Myeloid Leukemia

### Ziftomenib Triplet in R/R NPM1-Mutated AML: KOMET-007 Phase 1 Data

Ziftomenib, an oral selective menin inhibitor approved as monotherapy for R/R NPM1-mutated AML, was evaluated in combination with venetoclax and azacitidine in the KOMET-007 phase 1 trial (NCT05735184)[^2]. Sixty-seven patients with R/R NPM1-mutated AML were enrolled; the median age was 66 years and 55% were male. The median number of prior therapies was 1 (range 1–8), with 55% having received prior venetoclax-containing therapy and 22% having undergone prior transplantation. In the phase 1b expansion cohort at the recommended dose of ziftomenib 600 mg once daily, composite complete remission (CRc) was achieved in 46% of 48 patients (22/48), with 67% of centrally evaluated patients achieving MRD negativity at the <0.01% threshold (12/18). A striking difference was observed between venetoclax-naïve and venetoclax-exposed patients: CRc rates were 70% (16/23) and 24% (6/25), respectively, with corresponding MRD-negativity rates of 75% (9/12) and 50% (3/6). The median duration of response was 8.6 months and median OS was not reached. The most common grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse events were leukopenia (34%), thrombocytopenia (28%), febrile neutropenia and neutropenia (25% each). Differentiation syndrome occurred in only 2 patients (grade 3 in both), and QTc prolongation was seen in 6 patients (only 1 deemed ziftomenib-related, grade 1). These data represent the first clinical validation of menin inhibitor–based triplet therapy in this setting, and the depth of response in venetoclax-naïve patients is particularly notable.

### Overcoming Menin Inhibitor Resistance: BET and BRG1/BRM Combinatorial Strategies

A mechanistic study published in *Blood* this week addressed the emergence of menin inhibitor (MI) resistance in AML with MLL1 rearrangement (MLL1-r) or mutant NPM1 (mtNPM1)[^6]. Using repeated-shock methodology with the MI SNDX-50469 (a precursor tool compound to revumenib), investigators generated MI-resistant (MITR) MV4-11 and OCI-AML3 cell lines exhibiting an altered epigenome, transcriptome, and proteome without menin mutation. A CRISPR screen identified novel druggable co-dependencies including BRD4, SMARCA4 (BRG1), and CREBBP. Co-treatment with revumenib and the dual SMARCA4/SMARCA2 (BRG1/BRM) inhibitor FHD-286, or revumenib and the BET inhibitor OTX015 (birabresib), synergistically induced lethality in MITR cells, in cells expressing the resistance-conferring M327I menin mutation, and in patient-derived (PD) AML cells with ex vivo MI resistance. In xenograft models of MITR and MI-resistant PD MLL1-r AML cells, combined SNDX-5613 plus FHD-286 or OTX015 plus FHD-286 significantly reduced AML burden and improved survival versus each agent alone, without significant toxicity[^6]. A companion editorial contextualizes these findings within the broader challenge of menin inhibitor resistance management[^7].

### FLT3 + BCL2 Inhibitor Resistance: RAS Pathway as the Convergence Point

A multiomic single-cell study published in *Blood* this week dissects the resistance landscape to combined venetoclax and gilteritinib (Ven/Gilt) in a phase 1b clinical trial cohort (NCT03625505)[^8]. Single-cell DNA/protein and RNA/protein profiling revealed that while Ven/Gilt effectively eliminated FLT3-mutant clones, resistance was mediated through convergent RAS pathway activation via three mechanisms: selection of RAS-mutant clones, non-mutational upregulation of RAS transcriptional programs, and a shift to RAS-associated monocytic AML differentiation. Critically, the non-mutational mechanism — transcriptional RAS activation in the absence of acquired RAS mutation — would be invisible to conventional bulk sequencing. In an in vitro model of monocytic differentiation, RAS pathway inhibition re-sensitized cells to Ven/Gilt, supporting clinical evaluation of RAS-directed combinations in the post-Ven/Gilt relapse setting[^8].

### ELN-DAVID Recommendations for NGS-Based FLT3-ITD MRD Testing

The ELN-DAVID working group published companion technical recommendations for NGS-based FLT3-ITD MRD testing in AML in *Blood* this week[^9]. These guidelines complement recently updated clinical standard-of-care recommendations that formally endorsed FLT3-ITD MRD testing in patients in remission. The document provides laboratory-specific guidance on assay sensitivity thresholds, variant allele frequency reporting, and clinical interpretation frameworks, addressing the substantial inter-laboratory heterogeneity in FLT3-ITD MRD measurement that has limited its routine adoption[^9].

### AKT Inhibition Reverses EVI1-Driven Venetoclax Resistance

A translational study in *BJH* demonstrates that AML with MECOM rearrangement and high EVI1 expression exhibits significantly inferior complete remission rates to venetoclax-based regimens compared to EVI1-low disease[^10]. Mechanistically, elevated EVI1 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, stabilizing MCL-1 through increased phosphorylation at threonine 163 (T163pMCL-1), which attenuates ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated MCL-1 degradation and thereby confers venetoclax resistance. Critically, co-treatment with venetoclax and the clinically available AKT inhibitor capivasertib restored venetoclax sensitivity in both EVI1-high cell lines and primary patient-derived AML samples[^10]. Given that capivasertib is FDA-approved in other indications, this combination represents a near-term clinically testable strategy for a historically intractable subset.

### TP53-Deficient AML Resists T-Cell Engagers via an Immunosuppressive Secretome

A study in *Leukemia* this week reports that TP53-deleted or TP53-knockdown AML cells exhibit significantly reduced susceptibility to the CD3×CD33 bispecific T-cell engager AMG 330, with impaired T-cell proliferation and proinflammatory cytokine secretion in co-culture experiments[^11]. Proteomic analysis identified TGF-β1 in the secretome of TP53-knockdown AML cells as a key T-cell suppressor, and RNA sequencing of co-cultured T cells revealed a transcriptional shift toward a senescent cell-cycle profile. Transwell assays confirmed that the TP53-deficient secretome itself was sufficient to suppress T-cell function, establishing a cell-extrinsic immune evasion mechanism distinct from cell-intrinsic antigen loss. These findings directly inform the clinical challenge of developing T-cell engager strategies in TP53-mutated AML, which accounts for 10–15% of de novo and up to 25% of therapy-related disease[^11].

## II. ALL — Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

### γδTCR Expression in T-ALL: Two Distinct Developmental Subtypes

An investigator-initiated analysis published in *Blood* this week characterizes the molecular and clinical landscape of γδTCR-positive T-ALL across 1,233 T-ALL patients, identifying 191 γδTCR-positive cases (47% of TCR-positive T-ALLs; 113 adults, 78 children)[^12]. Two distinct subtypes were delineated. The first, comprising 75% of γδTCR+ cases, was characterized by ectopic homeodomain oncogene expression (HOXA9 or TLX3), Vβ-Jβ rearrangements, and a cortical thymocyte-like phenotype — termed "cortical-like γδ T-ALL." Transduction of T-cell progenitors with HOXA9 or TLX3 produced a differentiation bias toward γδTCR-expressing thymocytes, mechanistically explaining the subtype's apparent prevalence. The second "bona fide γδ T-ALL" subtype (25%) demonstrated phenotypic and transcriptional profiles consistent with native γδ thymocytes, was enriched for ETP-like and KMT2A-rearranged cases, and was associated with poor initial chemotherapy response but sensitivity to venetoclax — a therapeutically actionable finding validated in the COG AALL0434 cohort[^12].

### CNS Relapse in Adult ALL: Population-Based Swedish Registry Analysis

A population-based analysis of 838 adult ALL patients in Sweden from 2007 to 2022 published in *BJH* establishes the 5-year cumulative incidence of CNS relapse at 4.6% (95% CI 3.1–6.6)[^13]. CNS involvement at diagnosis was detected in 7.8% of patients, more frequently in T-ALL and those with WBC count >30 × 10⁹/L, but did not independently affect OS or systemic relapse. However, in B-ALL specifically, CNS involvement at diagnosis was associated with a fourfold increase in CNS relapse risk (HR 4.52; 95% CI 1.35–15.2; p<0.05), whereas no such association was observed in T-ALL (HR 0.94; 95% CI 0.12–7.53). Among patients experiencing CNS relapse, the 2-year OS was only 34% overall, falling to 13% when relapse occurred within 12 months of diagnosis. Neither Philadelphia chromosome status nor KMT2A::AFF1 rearrangement was associated with CNS relapse, suggesting that current risk stratification tools may be insufficient for CNS-directed therapy personalization[^13].

### Blinatumomab Maintenance After Allo-HCT in B-ALL: Long-Term Follow-Up

Long-term follow-up data for blinatumomab maintenance administered after allogeneic HCT in B-ALL were published in *Haematologica* this week, providing extended maturity data for this strategy in the post-transplant consolidation setting[^14]. Additionally, a separate *Haematologica* report describes the use of blinatumomab and ponatinib with CNS treatment intensification in adolescents and young adults (AYA) with Philadelphia-positive B-ALL, providing preliminary outcomes data for this chemotherapy-free platform in a traditionally high-risk population[^15].

## III. CML — Chronic Myeloid Leukemia

### 25 Years of TKI Therapy in CML: A Comprehensive Review

A landmark review published in *Leukemia* this week by Hochhaus, Talpaz, Saglio, Cortes, and colleagues traces the transformation of CML management from the imatinib era through the current landscape of treatment-free remission (TFR) and emerging PROTAC-based degrader approaches[^16]. The authors emphasize that approximately half of eligible patients can maintain TFR after structured discontinuation — a therapeutic endpoint that could not have been contemplated at the time of imatinib's introduction in 2001. Remaining challenges include TKI resistance in a minority of patients, cardiovascular and metabolic adverse effects of second- and third-generation TKIs, the limited proportion of patients achieving and sustaining TFR, and global treatment accessibility. The article contextualizes newer modalities including asciminib, ponatinib, degraders targeting BCR::ABL1, and immunomodulatory strategies as promising avenues to further expand TFR eligibility and address resistance[^16].

### TFR Prognostic Score Refinement

A technical communication in *BJH* provides further insights into the application of the TFR Prognostic Score (TPS) in CML patients attempting treatment-free remission, contributing additional real-world validation data and refining patient selection criteria for TKI discontinuation[^17].

## IV. CLL / SLL — Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia

### AI-Enhanced Flow Cytometry for MRD Detection in CLL

A study in *Leukemia* this week describes an artificial intelligence-enhanced flow cytometry pipeline that accelerates the identification of measurable residual CLL in samples with very low disease burden[^18]. The approach uses machine learning to classify CLL cells from normal B cells in multidimensional flow cytometry data, potentially addressing one of the major throughput and standardization barriers to routine MRD-guided therapy in CLL. While clinical validation in prospective trials is pending, this platform could meaningfully reduce time-to-MRD-result in the context of fixed-duration venetoclax-based regimens[^18].

## VI. MPN — Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (PV / ET / MF)

### NK Cells as Primary IFN-α Effectors in Polycythemia Vera

A mechanistic study published in *Blood* this week identifies NK cells as the principal immune effectors mediating IFN-α therapeutic activity in PV[^20]. Using a transgenic JAK2-V617F mouse model, investigators demonstrated that IFN-α induces expansion of CD27+ NK cells in the bone marrow, and that NK cell depletion abrogated the therapeutic effects of IFN-α. In patients with PV or ET receiving IFN-α therapy, the frequency of CD56bright NK cells was increased and correlated with molecular response. Mechanistically, NK cells preferentially killed JAK2-V617F mutant HSPCs in a TNF-α–dependent manner, independent of IFN-γ or NKG2D. Notably, PV mice lacking type-I IFN receptor on NK cells showed accelerated disease progression even without exogenous IFN-α, suggesting that basal type-I IFN sensing by NK cells constitutes an endogenous immune surveillance mechanism against MPN clones[^20]. These findings provide a rationale for NK cell activation strategies as a complement to IFN-α therapy in MPN.

### Non-Invasive Machine Learning Detection of Myelofibrosis Progression

A multicentre pilot study in *BJH* evaluated an interpretable machine learning model applied to peripheral blood parameters for the detection of moderate-to-severe myelofibrosis in 336 JAK2 V617F-positive MPN patients, with external validation in 92 additional patients[^21]. Among 10 machine learning methods assessed, a support vector machine model achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.916; 95% CI 0.83–0.99) with good calibration and clinical utility. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) interpretation identified hemoglobin level, IL-1β concentration, and patient age as the three most influential features. Preclinical experiments confirmed IL-1β upregulation with fibrosis progression in Jak2V617F knock-in mice, an effect reversed by ruxolitinib or pegylated interferon-α but not hydroxyurea. While the authors appropriately note that bone marrow biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, this blood-based tool offers a clinically meaningful non-invasive adjunct for monitoring fibrosis progression[^21].

## VII. DLBCL & Aggressive B-cell Lymphoma

### Predictive Biomarkers of Glofitamab Resistance: T-Cell Exhaustion and Tfh Cells

A comprehensive immune profiling study using patient-derived lymphoma spheroids (PDLS) from 39 R/R B-NHL samples identifies two immunological determinants of glofitamab resistance[^5]. High responders to glofitamab possessed CD8+ T cells with robust cytotoxic and activation gene signatures, whereas low responders showed enrichment of exhausted CD8+ T cells with high expression of TIGIT, LAG3, and PD-1. Additionally, low responders exhibited elevated functionally active CD4+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells in spatial proximity to malignant B cells, promoting B-cell survival through IL-21 and CXCL13 signaling pathways — a previously underappreciated immune-evasion axis. Analysis of pretreatment bulk RNA-sequencing data from 48 additional R/R B-NHL patients confirmed that high Tfh cell abundance was associated with poor glofitamab response. Mechanistically, Tfh cell depletion in the PDLS model increased B-cell killing, and anti-TIGIT co-treatment enhanced glofitamab efficacy in low-responder samples[^5]. These findings provide a biological rationale for checkpoint inhibitor combinations with glofitamab and lay groundwork for predictive biomarker-based patient selection.

### CREBBP Missense vs. Truncating Mutations: Differential Oncogenic Programs in FL and DLBCL

A mouse model study in *Blood* demonstrates that CREBBP missense mutations (specifically R1446H, affecting the lysine acetyltransferase domain) impose functionally distinct oncogenic programs compared to complete CREBBP loss[^22]. Using a conditional GC-specific CrebbpRH knockin model, the authors show that missense mutation — but not Crebbp truncation/loss — was sufficient to initiate malignant transformation, expanded GCs with abnormal architecture, increased T follicular helper cell percentages, and skewed immune responses toward memory B-cell differentiation. Furthermore, lymphoma cells with CREBBP missense vs. truncating mutations exhibited differential sensitivity to CREBBP/p300 small-molecule inhibitors, with potential therapeutic implications for precision selection between CREBBP-targeted agents in FL versus DLBCL[^22].

### HBV Co-Infection Management in DLBCL: Concurrent Antiviral Prophylaxis

A multicentre retrospective study in *BJH* evaluated concurrent versus sequential antiviral prophylaxis strategies for 112 chronic HBV-infected patients among 1,120 newly diagnosed DLBCL patients[^23]. Antivirals (principally entecavir) suppressed HBV DNA to undetectable levels in high-baseline-load patients (median 1.65 × 10⁶ IU/mL). The 3-year cumulative HBV reactivation rate was low across the entire cohort (4.2%), with no statistically significant difference between high-load (≥10⁴ IU/mL; 4.5%) and low-load (<10⁴ IU/mL; 4.1%) patients (p = 0.955). Three-year PFS (80.9% vs. 70.6%; p = 0.137) and complete remission rates (70.0% vs. 64.6%) were comparable between viral load strata. This study directly addresses a highly Asia-relevant clinical question — HBV-endemic populations including Taiwan have a substantial overlap between DLBCL incidence and chronic HBV carriage — and supports concurrent antiviral initiation with immunochemotherapy[^23].

## IX. Hodgkin Lymphoma & T-cell Lymphoma

### Nivolumab ± Vinblastine in Elderly Unfit HL: NIVINIHO Phase II

The LYSA NIVINIHO phase II trial reports outcomes of nivolumab-based treatment in 64 treatment-naive HL patients aged ≥61 years (median 75 years, range 62–91) with comorbidities precluding standard chemotherapy[^24]. After nivolumab induction, 11 patients (17.2%) achieved complete metabolic response (CMR) and were consolidated with nivolumab monotherapy; 23 patients (35.9%) with partial response or stable disease received vinblastine plus nivolumab consolidation. Among 56 evaluable patients, 16 (28.6%) achieved CMR at end of treatment. With a median follow-up of 24.4 months, median PFS was 9.8 months (95% CI 4.2–12.0) and the 2-year OS rate was 74.1% (95% CI 58.9–84.4%). Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 50% of patients, and nivolumab-related toxicity led to treatment discontinuation in 19 patients (29.7%). While demonstrating feasibility of checkpoint inhibitor-based treatment in this frail elderly population, the modest CMR rate underscores the need for better first-line options in HL patients who cannot tolerate standard ABVD-based regimens[^24].

### Pediatric Lymphoma and Immune Checkpoint Therapy: A Review

A comprehensive review in *Haematologica* synthesizes the evidence base for immune checkpoint therapy (ICT) across pediatric and adolescent lymphoma subtypes[^25]. In pediatric Hodgkin lymphoma, ICT has demonstrated strong efficacy in both high-risk and relapsed disease. Among subtypes with consistent PD-L1 expression — including primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL), anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), aggressive NK-cell lymphoma, and PTCL-NOS — the authors note that a randomized phase III trial of nivolumab combined with chemo-immunotherapy in newly diagnosed PMBCL (COG/NCTN cooperative group) has recently completed accrual, with results expected in 2027[^25].

## XI. Multiple Myeloma

### IMROZ MRD Dynamics: Isa-VRd Deepens and Sustains Responses Through 60 Months

A landmark MRD-dynamics analysis from the phase 3 IMROZ trial (NCT03319667), published in *Blood* this week, provides extended longitudinal data on 60 months of follow-up for transplant-ineligible NDMM patients randomized to Isa-VRd/Isa-Rd versus VRd/Rd[^3]. The analysis demonstrates that Isa-VRd achieved higher rates of MRD negativity and MRD-negative complete response at both end of induction and throughout maintenance compared to VRd. These benefits were consistently observed across key patient subgroups, including patients aged >70 years and those classified as frail. A particularly important translational finding is that time to progression (TTP) was significantly prolonged with Isa-VRd even in patients who converted from MRD-negative to MRD-positive at any time point during therapy — a group previously thought to have exhausted the benefit of deeper initial response. The authors suggest that serial MRD assessment at multiple time points should inform treatment continuation and switching decisions, a recommendation supported by the distinct TTP curves in landmark analyses at the time of MRD conversion[^3].

### Arlocabtagene Autoleucel (GPRC5D CAR T) in Heavily Pretreated RRMM

Phase 1 dose-escalation and expansion data for arlocabtagene autoleucel (arlo-cel, BMS-986393), a GPRC5D-targeting autologous CAR T-cell therapy, were published in *Blood* this week[^4]. Eighty-four patients with RRMM and ≥3 prior regimens (median 5 prior lines; 49% prior BCMA-targeted therapy, of whom 38% had prior CAR T) received arlo-cel at doses of 25×10⁶ to 450×10⁶ CAR T cells. At a median follow-up of 16.1 months, the ORR was 87%, CR rate was 53%, median duration of response was 18.0 months, median PFS was 18.3 months (95% CI 11.8–21.9), and 1-year OS rate was 90%. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in 82% of patients, ICANS in 10%, with most events being grade 1–2. On-target/off-tumor skin (30%), nail (19%), and oral adverse events (32%) were largely grade 1–2 and transient. One death occurred from CRS at the highest dose level. The maximum tolerated dose was not reached. Notably, approximately half of patients had previously received BCMA-targeted therapy, demonstrating efficacy across prior treatment exposures and suggesting non-overlapping mechanisms with BCMA-directed approaches[^4].

### PKMYT1 as a Therapeutic Target in del(17p) Myeloma

A translational study published in *Blood* this week identifies PKMYT1 — a Wee family kinase — as a synthetic lethal dependency in del(17p)/TP53-deficient multiple myeloma[^27]. Integration of RNA-seq data from patient MM cells with genetic dependency data from MM cell lines revealed PKMYT1 as preferentially essential in del(17p) MM. Pharmacological inhibition with the selective inhibitor RP-6306 triggered DNA damage accumulation, micronucleus formation, and mitotic catastrophe, resulting in preferential cell death in del(17p) MM cells while largely sparing del(17p)-negative MM cells and healthy cells. RP-6306 also reduced tumor burden and extended survival in both xenograft and TP53-deficient syngeneic models[^27]. Del(17p) myeloma represents one of the most adverse cytogenetic subgroups and has no molecularly targeted therapy; this study provides the first preclinical evidence for a biomarker-driven PKMYT1 inhibition approach in this unmet-need population.

### CSF-1R Inhibition Plus Lenalidomide Post-ASCT: A Synergistic Preclinical Strategy

A preclinical study in *Blood* identifies a CD64+CD169+CD163+ macrophage subset expressing CSF-1R, PD-L1, and CD155 that is expanded in patients who relapsed after ASCT[^28]. In a preclinical ASCT model, neither CSF-1R inhibition nor lenalidomide monotherapy alone significantly improved outcomes, but their combination synergistically attenuated disease progression and prolonged survival. Single-cell RNA-sequencing revealed that lenalidomide expanded NK-like CD8+ T cells but paradoxically increased CSF-1R+ macrophage frequency; CSF-1R blockade depleted these immunosuppressive macrophages, reducing inhibitory receptor expression and enhancing activation in effector T-cell populations. Given that axatilimab (a CSF-1R inhibitor) is FDA-approved for chronic GVHD, the authors argue this combination represents a readily testable clinical strategy[^28].

### Cesni-cel (ARI0002h) in Ultra-High-Risk Myeloma with PCL or CNS Involvement

A real-world series published in *BJH* reports outcomes of cesnicabtagene autoleucel (cesni-cel, ARI0002h), a BCMA-directed CAR T-cell therapy, in 17 patients with ultra-high-risk MM defined by plasma cell leukemia (PCL; n=12), CNS involvement (n=5), or both (n=2)[^29]. At day +100, ORR was 88%, with 87% achieving ≥VGPR and 80% achieving complete CNS response. Median PFS and OS were 10.5 and 15.9 months, respectively. CRS occurred in 76% of patients (none grade ≥3); one fatal case of IEC-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome (IEC-HS) was reported. These data provide the first meaningful series of CAR T outcomes specifically in the PCL/CNS-MM population — a group historically excluded from landmark CAR T trials — and support inclusion of these ultra-high-risk presentations in future clinical studies[^29].

### Ixazomib–Pomalidomide–Dexamethasone: Phase I/II All-Oral Regimen in RRMM

A phase I/II study in *Haematologica* evaluates twice-weekly ixazomib plus pomalidomide and dexamethasone in 50 RRMM patients with a median of 2 prior lines (98% prior lenalidomide, 88% prior bortezomib)[^30]. At the recommended phase II dose of ixazomib 4 mg twice weekly plus pomalidomide 4 mg, the ORR was 65.8% (≥VGPR 28.9%), median duration of response was 19.3 months, median PFS was 17.8 months, and 3-year OS was 80.3%. Common toxicities were neutropenia (76%), thrombocytopenia (70%), and leukopenia (68%), predominantly grade 1–2. The all-oral schedule presents a meaningful convenience advantage in the post-lenalidomide setting, particularly for patients with limited performance status or significant comorbidities[^30].
